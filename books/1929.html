BOOK CLUB — THURSDAY

Sorkin's method is to tell the crash through people, one dated chapter at a time. That works if you can hold the cast. If the names blur, the chapters stop connecting and 592 pages get long. Nothing below is from the book: it's the background the book takes for granted. Skim it now, keep it open, come back when a name stops meaning anything.

The cast

Grouped by house

Roughly twenty people carry the book. They're easier to hold as institutions than as individuals, because in 1929 the institution is the motive. Red flags mark the men who end up in front of a Senate committee, a jury, or both.

23 Wall Street · J.P. Morgan & Co.

Thomas W. Lamont

Senior partner. The operating head.

The most powerful private banker in America and the closest thing the country had to a central authority in a panic. He convened the bankers' pool at Morgan on the morning of Black Thursday. Afterward he told reporters there had been some distress selling on the exchange, which is one of the great understatements in financial history.

J.P. "Jack" Morgan Jr.

The name on the door.

Son of the man who personally stopped the Panic of 1907. He carried the family's authority without much of its appetite, and was often abroad. The gap between what the Morgan name promised in 1929 and what it could actually deliver is a live tension in the book.

George Whitney

Morgan partner. Richard Whitney's brother.

The wire between Morgan's back room and the floor of the exchange. He is also, years later, the reason his brother's embezzlement stayed quiet as long as it did.

National City Bank

Charles E. Mitchell

Chairman. "Sunshine Charlie."

The book's central figure and its best illustration of the conflicts nobody had outlawed yet. He ran the country's largest bank, sat as a director of the New York Fed, and through National City Company sold securities to the bank's own depositors. In March 1929, with the Fed trying to choke off speculation, he publicly committed National City's money to the call market and broke the squeeze himself.

Pecora · indicted 1933

Hugh Baker

President, National City Company.

Ran the sales force that pushed stock and foreign bonds to retail buyers, including Peruvian debt the bank's own analysts had flagged. The mechanics of that sales operation become the most damning testimony of the 1933 hearings.

Chase National Bank

Albert H. Wiggin

Chairman.

While publicly defending the market, he was short his own bank's stock through family-controlled companies, and cleared several million dollars on the way down. He then accepted a lifetime pension from Chase. No law he broke, which is precisely the point.

Pecora
The floor · New York Stock Exchange

Richard Whitney

Vice president, acting president in Oct 1929.

On Black Thursday he walked to the U.S. Steel post and bid 205 for 25,000 shares on behalf of the bankers' pool, a piece of theater that stopped the selling for a day. He became president of the exchange and its loudest opponent of federal regulation. In 1938 he went to Sing Sing for embezzlement.

Convicted 1938

Michael J. Meehan

Floor specialist in RCA.

Ran the RCA pool of March 1929, the era's showcase manipulation: accumulate quietly, trade among yourselves to manufacture volume, feed friendly columnists, sell into the crowd. Entirely legal. He later became the first person the SEC barred from the securities business.

Jesse Livermore

Speculator, on the short side.

The most famous bear of the age and a convenient villain whenever prices fell. Watch how quickly the press and the bankers reach for him as an explanation.

Washington and the Federal Reserve

Herbert Hoover

President from 4 March 1929.

Had worried about speculation privately for years while serving Coolidge, then inherited the bubble with eight months to go. Sorkin's Hoover is neither the do-nothing of the folk version nor a competent crisis manager, and how you read him is likely to be the liveliest argument at your table.

Andrew Mellon

Treasury Secretary, 1921–1932.

Served three presidents, was one of the richest men in the country, and cut taxes through the boom. The line Hoover later attributed to him about liquidating labor and stocks may or may not have been said; it shaped his reputation regardless.

Benjamin Strong

Governor, New York Fed. Died 16 Oct 1928.

He is dead before the book opens and haunts all of it. Strong had run American monetary policy in practice, and his death left the New York Fed and the Washington board fighting each other through the most consequential year either would ever face. One whole school of interpretation hangs on this.

George L. Harrison

Strong's successor at the New York Fed.

Wanted to raise rates early in 1929 and was blocked repeatedly by the board in Washington, which preferred to jawbone banks out of lending to speculators. That deadlock is the policy spine of the first half of the book.

Roy Young & Adolph Miller

Federal Reserve Board, Washington.

Architects of "direct pressure," the theory that the Fed could shame member banks away from funding speculation without touching the discount rate. It failed, and Mitchell humiliated it in public in March.

The trust builders

Waddill Catchings

Goldman Sachs.

Built Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation, then stacked Shenandoah on top of it in July 1929 and Blue Ridge on top of that in August, each trust holding the one below. Blue Ridge launched weeks before the peak. The leverage that made the returns spectacular on the way up made the wipeout total on the way down, and Goldman's reputation took twenty years to recover.

Samuel Insull

Chicago utilities.

Pyramided holding company on holding company until nobody, including Insull, could say what the assets were. The collapse in 1932 cost hundreds of thousands of small shareholders. He fled to Europe, was extradited, and was acquitted.

Ivar Kreuger

The Match King. Stockholm.

Ran a genuine global monopoly on top of forged assets and fictional books, and was a lender to sovereign governments. Shot himself in Paris in March 1932. His fraud is one of the largest of the century and forced the accounting reforms that followed.

Clarence Hatry

London financier.

His empire collapsed on 20 September 1929 over forged collateral, freezing British credit and forcing London investors to liquidate American holdings. One of the few genuinely proximate triggers, and it lands six weeks before Black Thursday.

The warnings, and the most famous wrong call

Roger Babson

Statistician and forecaster.

Told a conference on 5 September 1929 that a crash was coming and would be terrific. The market fell that afternoon: the Babson Break. He was mocked for weeks, including by economists with far better credentials.

Paul Warburg

Banker, architect of the Federal Reserve.

Warned in March 1929 of an orgy of unrestrained speculation and was accused of sandbagging American prosperity. The most technically qualified warning anyone gave, and it changed nothing.

Irving Fisher

Yale economist. The best in the country.

Said on 15 October 1929 that stocks had reached a permanently high plateau. He kept saying the recovery was imminent all the way down, was personally ruined, and spent the rest of his life building the debt-deflation theory that explains what happened to him.

Charles E. Merrill

Merrill Lynch.

Urged clients in early 1929 to get out of debt, then largely exited the brokerage business himself. The rare figure who saw it and acted on it.

The reckoning

Ferdinand Pecora

Counsel, Senate Banking Committee, from Jan 1933.

A Sicilian-born former New York prosecutor who put Mitchell, Wiggin and the Morgan partners under oath and made the details public. Nearly every securities law still in force traces to what came out of those ten days of hearings.

The machine

Seven mechanics the narrative assumes

Sorkin explains these as they arise, but in motion and in pieces. Having them cold beforehand means you can read the chapters as drama rather than as instruction.

Margin

Why the fall was total, not partial

By 1929 you could commonly buy stock with 10 to 20% down, sometimes less. Reg T later fixed it at 50%. The arithmetic is unforgiving: at 10% margin, a 10% decline erases the entire investment and the broker sells you out to protect his own loan.

This is why the market didn't drift down. Every leg lower manufactured forced sellers, and forced sellers made the next leg lower.

Call loans

The fuel, and the accelerant

Brokers funded margin accounts with overnight money callable on demand. Brokers' loans ran roughly $3.5B in 1927 and passed $8.5B by October 1929. With call rates hitting 12 to 20% against a 5% discount rate, corporations stopped building factories and lent to Wall Street instead. Foreign money poured in for the same reason.

The Fed's whole 1929 problem is here. Raising rates to stop speculation also strangled legitimate business; leaving them alone let the pile grow. Harrison wanted the first, Washington wanted neither.

Investment trusts

Leverage stacked on leverage

Closed-end funds, borrowed against, that bought other closed-end funds, which were also borrowed against. Three layers deep, a 10% move in the underlying stocks became a much larger move in the top-level shares. There was no requirement to disclose holdings, so buyers largely could not see what they owned.

Goldman's Trading Corporation, Shenandoah and Blue Ridge are the textbook case, launched in that order in the last months of the boom.

Pools

Legal manipulation

A syndicate accumulates a stock quietly, trades shares among its own members to create volume and the look of a trend, pays journalists to write it up, then distributes into the retail demand it manufactured. Nothing about this was illegal in 1929.

When you read about a stock "moving," ask who is moving it. Frequently the book has already told you.

The tape lag

The detail modern readers most underrate

Prices reached the world through mechanical tickers that could print only so fast. On 24 and 29 October the tape ran hours behind the floor. On Black Thursday it finished printing about four hours after the close.

So nobody knew the price. Not the public, not the brokers, not the specialists. People sold into a void because the alternative was holding a position whose value was unknowable. Information failure is what converted a decline into a panic, and it is the closest thing in the book to a mechanism you can point at.

The gold standard

Why 1930 and 1931 go the way they do

The dollar was convertible to gold at a fixed rate, so the Fed could not ease aggressively without risking outflows. Every central bank faced the same constraint, which made the contraction international and synchronized.

This matters more for the second half than the first. It's the reason a stock market crash could become a decade-long depression rather than a bad year.

What did not exist

The negative space of the whole book

No SEC. No mandatory disclosure of financial statements. No deposit insurance. No separation between commercial banking and securities underwriting, so a bank could sell its own bad underwriting to its own depositors. No prohibition on insider trading. No circuit breakers. No margin rules.

Read every scandal in the book against this list. Almost none of it was a crime at the time, which is why the second half is about writing laws rather than filing charges.

Three things to watch for while you read

  • Who knew. Sorkin's structure keeps putting private knowledge next to public statements. Wiggin shorting Chase while defending it, Mitchell lending against the Fed he sat on, Fisher doubling down all the way to ruin. Track whether each man is lying or genuinely believes it. The book is more interesting where the answer is the second one.
  • The crash versus the Depression. Sorkin's title says the crash shattered a nation, but the causal chain from October 1929 to 1933 is the most contested question in economic history. Watch how much weight he puts on the crash itself against monetary policy, the banking failures, and gold.
  • The present-day rhyme. He signals it in the jacket copy and threads it throughout. Decide for yourself whether the parallel earns its place or gets asserted.

The number to open with

25
Years before the Dow closed above its September 1929 peak again. November 1954. A buyer at the top who held on got even when Eisenhower was president.

If the conversation stalls Thursday, that's the fact that resets it. Everyone knows the market fell. Almost nobody has internalized how long it stayed down, and it reframes every argument about whether "this time is different."
