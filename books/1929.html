The number to open with

25
Years before the Dow closed above its September 1929 peak again. November 1954. A buyer at the top who held on got even when Eisenhower was president.

Three things to keep watching

  • Who knew. The structure keeps putting private knowledge next to public statements. Lamont raising cash in June and reassuring the president in October is the clean case so far. Track whether each man is lying or genuinely believes it, because the book is more interesting where it is the second one.
  • Nobody is breaking a law. Mitchell on the board of his own regulator, Meehan running a pool in the stock he makes the market in, Wiggin’s securities affiliate, the campaign notes, the preferred lists. None of it is a crime in 1929. The category had not been written.
  • The crash versus the Depression. The title says the crash shattered a nation, but the causal chain from October 1929 to 1933 is the most contested question in economic history. Watch how much weight lands on the crash itself against monetary policy, the banking failures, and gold.
  • The present-day rhyme. Signalled in the jacket copy and threaded throughout. Decide for yourself whether the parallel earns its place or gets asserted.

The machine

Seven mechanics the narrative assumes

These get explained as they arise, but in motion and in pieces. Having them cold means the chapters read as drama rather than instruction.

Margin

Why the fall was total, not partial

By 1929 you could commonly buy stock with 10 to 20% down, sometimes less. Reg T later fixed it at 50%. The arithmetic is unforgiving: at 10% margin, a 10% decline erases the entire investment and the broker sells you out to protect his own loan.

This is why the market did not drift down. Every leg lower manufactured forced sellers, and forced sellers made the next leg lower.

Call loans

The fuel, and the accelerant

Brokers funded margin accounts with overnight money callable on demand. Brokers' loans ran roughly $3.5B in 1927 and passed $8.5B by October 1929. With call rates hitting 12 to 20% against a 5% discount rate, corporations stopped building factories and lent to Wall Street instead. Foreign money poured in for the same reason.

The Fed's whole 1929 problem is here. Raising rates to stop speculation also strangled legitimate business; leaving them alone let the pile grow. Harrison wanted the first, Washington wanted neither.

Investment trusts

Leverage stacked on leverage

Closed-end funds, borrowed against, that bought other closed-end funds, which were also borrowed against. Three layers deep, a 10% move in the underlying stocks became a far larger move in the top-level shares. There was no requirement to disclose holdings, so buyers largely could not see what they owned.

Goldman's Trading Corporation, Shenandoah and Blue Ridge are the textbook case, launched in that order in the last months of the boom.

Pools

Legal manipulation

A syndicate accumulates a stock quietly, trades shares among its own members to create volume and the look of a trend, pays journalists to write it up, then distributes into the retail demand it manufactured. Nothing about this was illegal in 1929.

When you read that a stock is moving, ask who is moving it. Frequently the book has already told you.

The tape lag

The detail modern readers most underrate

Prices reached the world through mechanical tickers that could print only so fast. On 24 and 29 October the tape ran hours behind the floor, finishing on Black Thursday about four hours after the close.

So nobody knew the price. Not the public, not the brokers, not the specialists. People sold into a void because the alternative was holding a position whose value was unknowable. Information failure is what converted a decline into a panic, and it is the closest thing in the book to a mechanism you can point at.

The gold standard

Why 1930 and 1931 go the way they do

The dollar was convertible to gold at a fixed rate, so the Fed could not ease aggressively without risking outflows. Every central bank faced the same constraint, which made the contraction international and synchronised.

This matters more for the second half than the first. It is the reason a stock market crash could become a decade-long depression rather than a bad year.

What did not exist

The negative space of the whole book

No SEC. No mandatory disclosure of financial statements. No deposit insurance. No separation between commercial banking and securities underwriting, so a bank could sell its own bad underwriting to its own depositors. No prohibition on insider trading. No circuit breakers. No margin rules.

Read every scandal in the book against this list. Almost none of it was a crime at the time, which is why the second half is about writing laws rather than filing charges.

Terminology

Words the book uses without stopping

Roughly in the order you will hit them. Most of these are dead usages, which is why they slide past unnoticed: they look like ordinary English and are not.

The basics

Security
The umbrella word for a tradeable financial claim. A share of stock and a bond are both securities. Almost every institution and law in this book is named for the word: the securities affiliate, the Securities Act, the Securities and Exchange Commission. What they are all really about is who may create these claims, who may sell them, and what has to be told to the buyer.
Share, or stock
A piece of ownership in a company. It has no maturity and no promise of repayment. You make money if the company does well or if someone will pay you more than you paid, and you can lose all of it.
Bond
A loan, sliced into tradeable pieces. The issuer, a company or a government, pays interest on a schedule and returns the principal at the end. Safer than stock in principle, which is exactly why National City could sell foreign government bonds to schoolteachers, and why it mattered so much when some of those governments could not pay.
Issue, and underwriting
An issue is a batch of new securities sold to raise money. Underwriting is a bank agreeing to buy the whole batch from the issuer and resell it to the public, taking the risk and a fee. This is the business Glass-Steagall takes away from deposit-taking banks, because a bank that has already bought an issue has every reason to sell it to its own customers whatever it turns out to be worth.
Equity, debt
Two ways to finance anything. Equity is ownership and rides the outcome up and down. Debt is a fixed claim that must be paid regardless. Nearly every failure in this book is debt used to buy equity: margin, call loans, investment trusts, the Corn Exchange terms.

On the floor

Post
The fixed station on the exchange floor where one stock traded. "The Steel post."
Specialist
The broker assigned to make a market in that stock, obliged to buy when nobody else would. Meehan was the specialist in RCA.
The book
The specialist's ledger of unexecuted orders. He alone could see it, which meant he alone could see real demand.
Ticker, the tape
The telegraph printer carrying prices nationwide. The single most important piece of technology in the story.
Running behind
The tape printing later than the trades actually happening. The central failure of October.
Board room
The public room in a brokerage where customers watched a chalk quote board all day. Not a meeting room.
Board boy
The kid chalking prices onto that board. Livermore started as one.
Wire house
A brokerage with telegraph-linked branch offices. How the boom reached Kansas.
Bucket shop
A fake brokerage that pocketed the bet and never placed a trade. You were gambling against the house, not buying stock.
The Curb
The New York Curb Exchange, later the American Stock Exchange. It traded literally on the street until 1921.
Odd lot
Fewer than 100 shares. The small investor's trade, and a useful proxy for retail behaviour.
At the market
Sell at whatever price exists. The panic order.

Credit and leverage

Margin
The fraction of the purchase price the buyer actually put up. Often 10 to 20 percent.
Margin call
The broker demanding more cash as the collateral fell in value.
Sold out
The broker liquidating your position when the call went unmet. Involuntary, and immediate.
Call money
Overnight loans funding margin accounts, callable without notice by the lender.
The Money Post
The spot on the floor where the call rate was set each day at noon.
Brokers' loans
The weekly published total of that borrowing. Watched like a fever chart all through 1929.
Discount rate
What a Federal Reserve bank charged member banks to borrow from it. Harrison's weapon, refused ten times.
Direct pressure
Washington's alternative: shame banks out of lending rather than raise the rate. No enforcement behind it.
Pyramiding
Pledging paper gains as collateral to buy still more.

Positions and moves

Bull, bear
Long and short. Betting on the rise or the fall.
Short covering
Shorts buying back to close, which spikes the price and can look exactly like a recovery.
Bear raid
Coordinated shorting to break a stock. The standard accusation whenever prices fell, and rarely the actual cause.
Corner, squeeze
Trapping the shorts so they must buy back at any price you name.
A break
A sharp drop. "The March break." "The Babson break."
Liquidation
Forced selling. Note the passive framing: nobody chose it.
Technical rally
A bounce with no underlying improvement. What most of 1930 turned out to be.

Manipulation, all of it legal at the time

Pool
The syndicate. A pool operator runs it and takes a cut.
Matched orders, wash sales
Trading with yourself, or with a confederate, to manufacture volume.
Painting the tape
Making the ticker show activity that is not real demand.
Accumulation, distribution
Buying quietly, then unloading onto the public. The two halves of every pool.
Publicity man
The paid journalist. Meehan kept several.
Preferred list
Insiders offered new shares below the market price. When Pecora exposed Morgan's, it did more damage than any other single revelation.

Structures

Investment trust
A closed-end fund, leveraged, holdings undisclosed.
Securities affiliate
A commercial bank's underwriting and sales arm. National City Company, Chase Securities. Glass-Steagall exists to kill these.
Holding company
The vehicle for pyramiding control with very little capital. Insull's speciality.
Watered stock
Shares issued well beyond any real asset value behind them.
Suspension
What banks did instead of failing. A euphemism that let a run look temporary.

Two that mislead

Worth correcting before Thursday

Black Thursday was not the worst day. The market closed 24 October down only about 2 percent. Monday and Tuesday were far worse. Thursday earned the name because of the volume, the tape lag and the bankers' rescue, not the loss.

Short selling did not cause it. Bear raids get blamed constantly in 1929 rhetoric, by the press and by the bankers, and there is no good evidence they drove the decline. Watch how quickly Livermore becomes the explanation whenever anyone needs one.

The Federal Reserve

What it is, and why it could not act

It was created in 1913 for one reason. The Panic of 1907 had been stopped by Pierpont Morgan personally, locking bankers in his library until they agreed to a rescue, and the country decided it could not keep depending on one man's balance sheet and mood. So Congress built an institution to do what Morgan had done. Then, not trusting a central bank, it built a divided one.

Read the bottom arrow first

The part that shocks modern eyes

The regional reserve banks are owned by the commercial banks in their district, and those banks elect some of the directors. A Class A director is a banker chosen by bankers. That is how the head of the largest bank in America came to sit on the board of the institution supervising him, and why nobody in February 1929 thought it improper.

Three levers, in descending order of teeth

And why the weakest one was chosen

The discount rate is the price the Fed charges banks to borrow from it. Raise it and credit gets dearer everywhere, including the call money funding margin accounts. This is the real weapon, and it is the one New York asked for ten times without success.

Open market operations means buying or selling government securities to add or drain cash from the banking system. Quieter and slower.

Direct pressure means letters and speeches telling banks to stop lending to speculators. No enforcement. This is what Washington chose, and it is what Mitchell broke in public in March.

Washington's refusal was not stupidity. A rate rise hits the whole country, not just the exchange. Roy Young's argument was that raising New York's rate would drag every bank east of the Mississippi with it and crush farmers already struggling, in order to discipline speculators in Manhattan. He wanted a tool that could punish Wall Street without punishing Kansas. No such tool existed. Direct pressure was the fantasy that one did.

Fifteen years old

Nobody had settled who was in charge

The system had no established answer to whether Washington or New York ran policy. Benjamin Strong had answered it in practice, by force of personality, running things from New York whatever the organisation chart said. He died in October 1928 and the question reopened at the worst possible moment.

The geography is not decoration. It is why the Board kept saying no: a single national rate had to serve both the call market in Manhattan and the wheat harvest in the Dakotas. The banks

Founding to 21 June 1933

Every institution in the book, from its founding to the week the rules changed. An open dot marks the founding year. A red dot marks one of the six houses whose bankers met at 23 Wall Street on Black Thursday. Everything else here is somebody they bought, somebody they could not buy, or somebody who was not invited.

× ends a line. The Bank of United States failed in December 1930. National City Company and Chase Securities were the securities affiliates, wound up under the Banking Act. The dashed stub at 1930 is the Corn Exchange merger Mitchell could not complete. Every surviving line runs to 21 June 1933, five days after Roosevelt signed.

Almost nothing ends

Which is the point

Nearly every line runs flush to the right edge, because on that date they all still existed. The reckoning had only just been signed and none of it had happened yet. Three lines stop early, and they are the whole story: the Bank of United States in December 1930, and then National City Company and Chase Securities, the two securities affiliates, ended by law within days of each other in 1933. Mitchell’s machine and Wiggin’s machine, switched off by the same sentence.

Notice the ages. National City was founded in 1812, Chemical in 1824, Mechanics and Metals in 1810. These were century-old institutions by the time the book opens. The youngest thing on the chart is the Bank of United States, chartered in 1913, and it is the one that fails.

The Chase run

Four banks in five years

Mechanics and Metals in 1926, Garfield and National Park in 1929, Equitable Trust in 1930. Four institutions, the oldest founded in 1810, all folded into Chase inside five years, which is how Wiggin ends up running the largest bank in the world at the exact moment Mitchell’s own merger collapses.

Read the Corn Exchange stub against it. The dashed line going nowhere in 1930 is the deal that would have made National City the largest instead.

The laws

What existed, what moved, what came after

The book is not about crimes. Almost nothing in it was illegal at the time, which only makes sense once you can see what the statute book actually contained in 1929 and what it did not.

In force when the book opens

1890Sherman Antitrust Act
The main federal check on combination, aimed at monopoly in trade. It says nothing whatever about how securities are sold, who may sell them, or what must be disclosed.
1911 onwardState blue sky laws
A patchwork of state securities statutes beginning in Kansas. Enforcement varied by state and stopped at the state line. There was no federal equivalent of any kind.
1913Federal Reserve Act
Glass's own. Twelve regional reserve banks owned by their member banks, supervised by an appointed Board in Washington. The division was a deliberate political compromise, and it is the reason nobody can act in 1929.
1914Clayton Antitrust Act
Barred interlocking directorates between competing corporations. It does not reach a bank chairman sitting on the board of the institution that regulates him, which is why Mitchell's position is legal.
1919Volstead Act, and the Eighteenth Amendment
Prohibition. The issue Raskob bet the 1928 election on, and the subject Hoover devotes most of his inaugural address to.
1921–1926The Mellon revenue acts
Three rounds of cuts taking the top marginal rate from 73 percent to 25. More capital in fewer hands, at the same moment the call market began paying 12 to 20 percent.
1925Federal Corrupt Practices Act
Required disclosure of campaign contributions and provided essentially no enforcement. It is why the notes and pledges behind the 1928 campaign are invisible, and why the debt afterward is a private matter between rich men and their banks.
1927McFadden Act
Restricted branch banking, and gave national banks explicit authority to deal in investment securities. This is the law that legitimised the securities affiliate, meaning National City Company and Chase Securities. Glass-Steagall exists to undo it.
1927Radio Act, the White Act
Created the Federal Radio Commission and the public interest standard for licences. Section 17 bars a radio company from combining with a cable or telegraph company where the union would lessen competition. The single obstacle to the RCA and ITT deal Lamont is building.

Moving during 1929

15 Apr 1929The special session
Hoover calls Congress back for two things: farm relief, and what he describes as limited tariff revision. Both grow.
15 Jun 1929Agricultural Marketing Act
The farm relief half. Creates the Federal Farm Board with a revolving fund to stabilise crop prices. It is overwhelmed within two years.
1929–1930The Hawley-Smoot tariff bill
Willis Hawley in the House and Reed Smoot in the Senate. The limited revision becomes a general increase through logrolling: every member wants protection for his own district and trades votes to get it. It takes fourteen months, and the market watches the whole way. Signed 17 June 1930 over a petition from more than a thousand economists.
1929Glass's speculation tax
A proposed penalty on profits from stock held only briefly, meant to make rapid turnover expensive enough to discourage it. It is the same instinct as his demand that Mitchell resign: if the Fed cannot restrain speculation, make speculating cost something. Not enacted.
Jun 1929The Young Plan
Not an American law but the product of the Paris committee. It cuts Germany's annual reparations, sets an end date, and creates the Bank for International Settlements.
What 1929 produced

The legislative aftermath, through the reforms that governed American finance for the next sixty-six years. History rather than plot, but it does tell you where the book lands.

17 Jun 1930Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act
Raises duties on more than twenty thousand goods. Retaliation follows and world trade contracts sharply. How much of the Depression it caused, as against amplified, is still argued.
Jan 1932Reconstruction Finance Corporation Act
Federal lending to banks and railroads. Hoover's largest intervention, and the template for a great deal of what follows him.
27 Feb 1932Glass-Steagall Act of 1932
The other one with the same name. It widened what the Fed could accept as collateral against currency. Useful to know it exists, so the 1933 act does not confuse you.
9 Mar 1933Emergency Banking Act
Passed in a single day to reopen the banks after the national holiday.
27 May 1933Securities Act of 1933
Mandatory disclosure for new issues, with liability for misstatement. The direct answer to the National City sales force.
16 Jun 1933Banking Act of 1933, Glass-Steagall
Separates commercial from investment banking, killing the securities affiliates outright, and creates federal deposit insurance. Glass wanted the separation and opposed the insurance; Steagall would not move without it.
30 Jan 1934Gold Reserve Act
Nationalises monetary gold and revalues the dollar. The gold constraint that shaped 1930 and 1931 is finally cut.
6 Jun 1934Securities Exchange Act of 1934
Creates the SEC, outlaws matched orders and wash sales, requires ongoing reporting, and gives the Fed authority over margin, which becomes Regulation T. Every mechanic in the section above is addressed here.
1935Public Utility Holding Company Act
Breaks up the pyramided utility holding companies. The answer to Insull.
1940Investment Company Act
Regulates the funds that were called investment trusts in 1929. The answer to Shenandoah and Blue Ridge.
1999Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act
Repeals the separation of commercial and investment banking. Whether that mattered is the argument the book's final pages are inviting.
