Aversion by Fraternity

ANOVA F = 10.29  ·  p < 0.0001  ·  27 organizations with n ≥ 40

All 27 organizations with 40 or more survey respondents, sorted from lowest to highest aversion. Blue bars extend left (less averse); orange bars extend right (more averse). Zero is the full joiner sample mean.

Organization Less Averse  /  More Averse Score n

Differences across fraternities are statistically significant but the practical spread is modest. Kappa Alpha Order (mean -0.51, n = 1,053) and Sigma Alpha Epsilon (-0.26, n = 3,398) hold large, reliable samples well below zero. Pi Kappa Alpha is the largest single sample (n = 8,977) and sits at essentially zero (+0.008), making it the closest thing to a fraternity-wide baseline.

ATO (+0.27, n = 3,427) and Phi Kappa Psi (+0.21, n = 1,644) are large enough that these scores reflect structural patterns in who is joining, not sampling variance. Kappa Alpha Psi (n = 111, mean -0.18) is a historically Black fraternity more enthusiastic than most historically White fraternities of comparable size, which runs counter to the racial demographic aggregate where non-White groups score substantially higher on aversion.

Highest aversion
Phi Kappa Tau (+0.55) is the highest-aversion fraternity with a qualifying sample. Delta Sigma Phi (+0.35), ATO (+0.27), and Beta Theta Pi (+0.28) follow. These are structural, not statistical noise.
Most enthusiastic
Delta Kappa Epsilon (-0.56), Kappa Alpha Order (-0.51), and Sigma Nu (-0.46) lead. KA Order and SAE are also two of the three largest samples in the dataset -- reliable signal, not small-n artifacts.
Notable: Kappa Alpha Psi
Mean -0.18 with n = 111. More enthusiastic than most historically White fraternities of comparable size, despite racial demographics predicting the opposite in aggregate.
02

Reasons for Joining

ANOVA F = 14.65  ·  p < 0.0001  ·  variables external to aversion score construction

Two views of the data: the single most important reason each student named for joining, and the aversion gradient across importance levels for four key reasons. These variables are independent of the concern items that make up the aversion score.

Primary Reason for Joining Less Averse  /  More Averse Score n

Family legacy (-0.42, n = 381) produces the most enthusiastic joiners by a wide margin. Professional networking (-0.02) and campus leadership (-0.04) also land below zero. Finding a place to belong (+0.19, n = 7,992) is the second most common primary reason and the largest aversion-positive group by count.

Encouraged by a friend (+0.47, n = 1,521) and scholarship money (+0.87, n = 216) are the highest-aversion primary motivations. The gap between Family Legacy (-0.42) and Encouraged by a Friend (+0.47) is a 0.89-unit spread -- the largest in this section. Recruitment leaning on peer pressure is pulling in the most conflicted members.

Average aversion at each importance level: 1 = Not at all important  ·  4 = Very important. Sense of Belonging and Professional Networking are omitted -- their near-zero r values and non-monotonic patterns mean there is no interpretable gradient.

Family Encouragement shows the steepest gradient (r = -0.120): students rating it "very important" average -0.42 versus +0.33 for "not important" -- a 0.74-unit spread. Family Legacy is nearly as sharp (r = -0.092): "very important" averages -0.45. Community Service and Campus Leadership both show clean negative gradients as motivation intensifies.

03

Why They Chose This College

All correlations p < 0.001 unless noted  ·  n approximately 30,000 per item

School selection motivation is the sharpest external predictor of aversion in the dataset. Students who factored Greek life into their college decision arrive having already made peace with it. Students who chose primarily for cost or academic prestige arrive with the same aversion profile as the broader non-joiner population.

Primary School Choice Reason Less Averse  /  More Averse Score n

Greek or sorority life as the primary choice factor produces an average aversion of -0.77 (n = 485) -- the single most aversion-negative segment in this section. Athletic reputation (-0.26) follows. Cost as primary reason (+0.42, n = 4,265) is the second-highest aversion group.

The gap between Greek Life as primary reason (-0.77) and Cost as primary reason (+0.42) is 1.19 units -- the largest single contrast in the college choice data. Students who chose their school as a financial decision rather than a social one are arriving at fraternity membership through a very different motivational pathway.

Average aversion at each importance level: 1 = Not at all important  ·  4 = Very important.

The Greek Life gradient is the steepest (r = -0.112): "very important" averages -0.59 versus +0.26 for "not at all important" -- a 0.85-unit swing. Cost runs opposite (r = +0.169): "very important" averages +0.54 versus -0.55 for "not at all important" -- a 1.09-unit spread, the largest raw gap in the college choice section. Academic Reputation (r = +0.083) steps from -0.44 to +0.25 across the importance scale.

04

Two Pipelines Into Membership

Fraternity, join motivation, and college choice converge on a single structural finding. There are two distinct pathways into Greek membership, and they produce meaningfully different members.

Pipeline A: Self-Selected
Student chose school partly for Greek life, comes from a Greek family, and joins with purpose -- legacy, leadership, or professional goals. Arrives below zero on aversion. Concerns were resolved before bid day. Most likely in KA Order, SAE, or Phi Gamma Delta. This pipeline produces the most stable, committed members.
Pipeline B: Socially or Financially Routed
Student chose school for cost or academic reputation and joins because a friend encouraged them or for scholarship money. Arrives above zero -- concerns were not resolved before joining. Most likely in ATO, Phi Kappa Psi, or Phi Kappa Tau. This pipeline is larger by count and more at risk for attrition and engagement issues.
The recruitment implication
Addressing aversion after bid acceptance is too late for Pipeline B members. The divergence begins at school selection and is largely established before recruitment starts. Interventions that reach cost-constrained or academically-oriented students earlier in their college experience operate upstream of where current recruitment efforts focus.
