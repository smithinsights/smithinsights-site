Chapter Intelligence Primer

June 2026  ·  Director of Systems  ·  Internal Reference

This document explains every indicator used in the Chapter Intelligence dashboard. Each indicator includes what it measures, why it matters, what the data shows across ATO chapters, and the conversation it drives. No statistical methodology is included. Benchmarks are drawn from four academic years of Chapter Operations Survey data matched against award history and chapter closure records.

How to Read Dashboard Values

Each chapter's dashboard shows values from their most recent Fall and Spring Chapter Operations Survey. A "Partial" response means the chapter answered yes in one term and no in the other -- that inconsistency is itself a discussion point. Numeric questions are averaged across both terms. Member counts are rounded to the nearest whole person. Database metrics (chapter size, pledge class size, depledge rate) are drawn from re:members and reflect the most recent available snapshot.

Zone 1
Baseline Operations
These indicators predict both True Merit and chapter closure. When anything here is flagged, that conversation comes first.

These ten indicators predict both True Merit achievement and chapter closure. A chapter missing any of them is simultaneously falling short of excellence and showing early signs of organizational stress. When something in this zone is orange, that conversation takes priority over everything else on the dashboard. These are not aspirational targets. They are table stakes for a functioning chapter.

Bible Study or Devotional
True Merit chapters: 76% Non-TM: 35% Closing chapters: 12%

A standing program where chapter members gather for spiritual reflection, Scripture discussion, or devotional practice. It does not need to be led by a campus minister or tied to a specific faith tradition. It needs to be consistent and chapter-owned.

This is the single strongest predictor of True Merit in the dataset and one of the sharpest dividers between chapters that close and chapters that thrive. It is not a religiosity signal -- it is a brotherhood identity signal. Chapters that hold regular devotionals have built a reason for members to gather beyond chapter business and social events. That connective tissue sustains a chapter through roster turnover, difficult semesters, and leadership transitions.

When it is orange

Ask the chapter president when the chapter last met for something other than a business meeting or a social event. If the answer is vague, the chapter lacks the identity infrastructure that sustains long-term health. The conversation is not about religion -- it is about whether the chapter has built a shared reason to exist beyond programming.

Judicial Board Size
True Merit avg: 9 members Non-TM avg: 8 Closing avg: 7

The number of initiated members actively serving on the Judicial Board. The Judicial Board handles member accountability, standards enforcement, and chapter discipline. Its size reflects how seriously the chapter has built the infrastructure to govern itself. A board with fewer than seven members is typically too thin to function as an independent check on chapter behavior.

Closing chapters average seven members. True Merit chapters average nine. The gap is not dramatic, but it is consistent: chapters that are declining are quietly allowing their accountability structures to atrophy. A small Judicial Board often means the board rarely convenes, rarely hears cases, and rarely enforces standards.

When it is orange

Ask how many men are actively serving on the board and when it last met. A board that exists on paper but does not convene is not functioning. Follow with whether the board has heard a case in the past eighteen months.

Committee Count
True Merit avg: 8 committees Non-TM avg: 6 Closing avg: 4

The number of active committees currently operating within the chapter -- not committees that exist in the bylaws, but committees that are meeting and doing work. Committees are the mechanism by which chapter work gets distributed beyond the Executive Board. A chapter with four or fewer committees is concentrating operational responsibility in a handful of officers, which creates fragility and limits how many members are meaningfully involved in chapter leadership.

Chapters that close average four committees. True Merit chapters average eight. More committees means more members with ownership of chapter functions and more resilience when individual officers underperform or depart mid-year.

When it is orange

Ask the president to name the active committees and who chairs each one. If he hesitates or describes committees that have not met recently, the chapter is operating from a smaller leadership base than it should be. The goal is not committees for their own sake -- it is ensuring that chapter functions are distributed and that more than five or six men feel ownership of how the chapter operates.

Written Recruitment Goal
True Merit avg: 36 men/year Non-TM avg: 23 Closing avg: 16

The number the chapter has written down as its target for new member recruitment in the current academic year. The goal itself matters less than whether one exists and whether the chapter is working toward it. Chapters without a written goal tend to recruit reactively, and reactive recruitment produces inconsistent class sizes and unpredictable chapter health year to year.

True Merit chapters set goals that average 36 men per year -- more than double the 16-man average for chapters that go on to close. A written goal signals that chapter leadership has thought concretely about growth, assigned ownership to a recruitment structure, and set a standard they will be held to.

When it is orange

Ask what the specific recruitment goal is for this academic year, who owns the plan to hit it, and how many men have pledged so far relative to that goal. A chapter that cannot answer the first question has not treated recruitment as a managed function.

Full Set of Ritual Equipment
True Merit chapters: 84% Non-TM: 70% Closing chapters: 44%

The chapter has a complete, usable set of Ritual equipment -- altar, candles, collar pins, Ritual books, and associated materials. This is both a practical requirement for conducting initiations and a signal of how the chapter treats its identity as an ATO chapter.

Fewer than half of chapters that go on to close have a full equipment set. A chapter without its Ritual materials has allowed its connection to the founding principles of the organization to physically deteriorate. Equipment that is missing, broken, or stored improperly reflects the same organizational drift that shows up elsewhere in the data.

When it is orange

Ask when the chapter last conducted a formal initiation and what equipment was present. Determine what is missing and why. Missing equipment that has not been replaced across multiple semesters is worth noting beyond the equipment itself.

BOT Meets Quarterly
True Merit chapters: 85% Non-TM: 65% Closing chapters: 33%

The Board of Trustees meets with the chapter at least four times per academic year. Quarterly meetings are the minimum frequency for a BOT to remain engaged with chapter operations, aware of emerging issues, and useful to chapter leadership as an advisory body.

Only one in three closing chapters meets this standard. Among True Merit chapters, the rate is 85 percent. A BOT that meets less than quarterly has effectively disengaged from chapter oversight. It may still exist, but it is not providing the external accountability and institutional memory the BOT structure is designed to provide.

When it is orange

Ask when the BOT last met, how many members attended, and whether the chapter president was present. A BOT that has not met in more than four months should be treated as inactive regardless of what the chapter reports on the survey.

Scholarship Committee
True Merit chapters: 61% Non-TM: 46% Closing chapters: 26%

The chapter has an active Scholarship Committee -- a standing group of members responsible for monitoring academic performance, supporting members who are struggling, and enforcing academic standards for membership in good standing. A committee is distinct from having a scholarship chairman: it means multiple members are engaged in the academic welfare function.

Chapters that close are roughly half as likely to have this structure as True Merit chapters. A chapter without a Scholarship Committee is leaving its academic mission to informal enforcement and voluntary compliance, which rarely holds up under pressure.

When it is orange

Ask who chairs the committee, how many members serve on it, and when it last met as a body. Then ask how the chapter identifies members who are academically at risk before they fall below the membership standard.

Two or More Alumni Events Per Year
True Merit chapters: 79% Non-TM: 60% Closing chapters: 33%

The chapter organizes and hosts at least two structured alumni events per academic year -- events designed to bring alumni back to the chapter, not just invite them to social functions. Alumni events are the primary mechanism by which chapters maintain the relational network that supports BOT effectiveness, emergency fundraising, mentorship, and long-term chapter health.

Closing chapters host alumni events at half the rate of chapters that remain open. Rebuilding alumni relationships after they have lapsed is significantly harder than maintaining them.

When it is orange

Ask what alumni events have been held in the past twelve months, how many alumni attended each, and what alumni events are planned for the coming semester. A chapter describing social events that alumni happen to attend is not the same as a chapter that has organized alumni-specific programming.

IFC Officer or Chair
True Merit chapters: 73% Non-TM: 65% Closing chapters: 60%

At least one chapter member holds an officer or committee chair position within the campus Interfraternity Council. IFC involvement signals that the chapter is an active participant in the broader campus Greek system, which affects the chapter's standing with the institution, its relationships with other fraternities, and the professional and leadership development of individual members.

While the gap between True Merit and non-TM chapters on this indicator is smaller than others, the closure data is more meaningful: chapters that disengage from IFC tend to be withdrawing from campus life more broadly. IFC participation is often among the first forms of external engagement to decline as a chapter struggles.

When it is orange

Ask which members currently hold IFC positions and whether the chapter attends IFC meetings consistently. A chapter that is present at IFC but not represented in leadership is a different situation from a chapter that has stopped attending altogether.

Zone 2
True Merit
Disciplined chapters pass Zone 1. Exceptional chapters go further. These indicators separate chapters that are running well from chapters that are compounding specific disciplines into something exceptional.

Disciplined chapters pass the Baseline Operations checks. Exceptional chapters go further. The difference is not one dramatic leap -- it is the accumulation of specific, sustained practices that compound over time. A chapter below a benchmark here is not failing; it is operating at a level that produces adequate outcomes but not exceptional ones. Each indicator in this section represents a concrete practice that distinguishes chapters earning the organization's highest recognition from chapters that are simply running well. The benchmarks shown are averages from chapters that won True Merit in the past four academic years.

Ritual & Identity
Fraternity Songs Taught to All Members
True Merit chapters: 86% Non-TM: 65%

The chapter actively teaches ATO Ritual songs to all initiated members, not just those present at specific events. This captures whether the chapter has made musical and ceremonial tradition a shared experience across the entire membership rather than something only older brothers know.

True Merit chapters do this at a 21-point higher rate than non-TM chapters. It is a proxy for how intentionally the chapter transmits its culture to new members. Chapters where songs are known only by a core group of older brothers have a cultural transmission problem that extends well beyond music.

Conversation

Ask when and how the chapter teaches songs to new initiates, and whether all members could sing them at a chapter event. If the answer involves a single event or a subset of members, the chapter is treating cultural transmission as optional.

Distinguished Alumnus at Each Initiation
True Merit chapters: 86% Non-TM: 71%

A Distinguished Alumnus is present and participates in each initiation ceremony. This connects new members to the chapter's alumni tradition on the day they become initiated brothers and signals that initiation is a significant enough occasion to bring alumni back for it.

The 15-point gap between TM and non-TM chapters is consistent across all four years of data. It is not just about ceremony -- it is about whether the chapter treats initiation as a moment worthy of institutional investment.

Conversation

Ask who was present at the most recent initiation and what role alumni played. If alumni attendance is inconsistent or depends on who the chapter president happens to know personally, the chapter has not built the alumni infrastructure to sustain this practice reliably.

Average Alumni at Initiation
True Merit avg: 3 alumni Non-TM avg: 2

Beyond whether a Distinguished Alumnus is present, this measures how many alumni attend initiation ceremonies on average. True Merit chapters bring roughly three alumni to each initiation; non-TM chapters average two. The difference reflects the depth of the alumni relationship network the chapter has built and how actively it cultivates alumni participation in its most significant events.

Conversation

Ask how many alumni attended the most recent initiation and how the chapter communicated the date and invitation. A chapter that cannot reliably bring more than one alumni to an initiation is working from a thin alumni contact list.

Ritual Equipment Secured from Non-Members
True Merit chapters: 83% Non-TM: 76%

Ritual equipment and books are stored in a location secured from access by non-initiated members and non-members. This is both a practical requirement of ritual integrity and a signal of how seriously the chapter treats the confidential nature of its ceremonial practices.

Chapters that do not secure their equipment are allowing the Ritual to become casual -- accessible to anyone in the house and stripped of the significance that comes from being something only initiated brothers hold.

Conversation

Ask where equipment is stored and who has access. If the answer involves a shared closet, an unlocked room, or informal storage, the chapter is not protecting what the Ritual is designed to be.

Governance & Accountability
Bylaws Updated This Year
True Merit chapters: 91% Non-TM: 73%

The chapter has reviewed and updated its bylaws within the current academic year. Annual bylaw review ensures that the governing document reflects the chapter's actual operations, that outdated provisions have been removed, and that the chapter's standards are formally current.

Chapters whose bylaws have not been updated in multiple years are operating under rules that no longer match how the chapter functions. That misalignment creates ambiguity in accountability situations. True Merit chapters update at a 91 percent rate -- it is effectively a standard, not an aspiration.

Conversation

Ask when the bylaws were last updated and what changed. A chapter that cannot name a specific change from the most recent revision is likely going through the motions of review without substantive engagement.

BOT Is Operational
True Merit chapters: 93% Non-TM: 67%

The Board of Trustees is actively functioning -- it has defined membership, meets regularly, and is engaged with chapter operations. This is distinct from a BOT that exists on paper only. An operational BOT is one that the chapter president can describe in terms of who serves on it, when it meets, and what it does.

The 26-point gap between TM and non-TM chapters is one of the largest in this section. A non-functional BOT removes the primary external accountability structure from the chapter's governance ecosystem. The chapter is effectively self-governing without oversight, which works when leadership is strong and fails badly when it is not.

Conversation

Ask who currently chairs the BOT, when it last met with the chapter, and what was discussed. Vague or uncertain answers indicate the BOT is not meaningfully engaged.

BOT Size
True Merit avg: 7 members Non-TM avg: 5

The number of members actively serving on the Board of Trustees. True Merit chapters average seven BOT members; non-TM chapters average five. A larger BOT brings more institutional memory, broader alumni networks, more capacity for ongoing chapter engagement, and greater resilience when individual members cycle off.

A BOT with three or four members is functioning at the edge of its capacity. A single member departing can make the board functionally inactive. Building BOT size is a long-term process, but chapters that have invested in it consistently outperform those that have not.

Conversation

Ask how many members are actively serving on the BOT today. Then ask when the BOT last recruited a new member. Stagnant BOT membership is a slow-moving risk that typically becomes visible only in a crisis.

Budget Reports to BOT
True Merit chapters: 85% Non-TM: 54%

The chapter provides budget reports to its Board of Trustees on a regular basis. Financial transparency with the BOT is the mechanism by which alumni oversight functions as a meaningful check on chapter spending and financial health. True Merit chapters treat the BOT as an active governance partner, not a ceremonial body. Budget reporting is the most concrete expression of that partnership.

Conversation

Ask how often budget reports go to the BOT and what format they take. If the chapter treasurer has not sent anything to the BOT in the past semester, the financial transparency function has broken down regardless of what the chapter reports on the survey.

Judicial Board Expelled a Member in the Last 18 Months
True Merit chapters: 70% Non-TM: 47%

The Judicial Board has formally expelled at least one initiated member within the past eighteen months. This is not a measure of how many problems the chapter has -- it is a measure of whether the chapter's accountability structures are functioning. Chapters that never expel anyone are not necessarily problem-free; they are often chapters that avoid enforcing their own standards.

True Merit chapters expel members at nearly double the rate of non-TM chapters. A Judicial Board that has heard a case and rendered a consequential decision is a board that is operational, that members take seriously, and that the chapter uses as a real governance tool.

Conversation

Ask when the Judicial Board last convened to hear a case and what the outcome was. A board that has not heard a case in eighteen months should prompt a question about whether the chapter has had zero legitimate standards violations or is avoiding the process.

Outside Financial Audit
True Merit chapters: 46% Non-TM: 38%

An outside financial audit is conducted by someone outside the active chapter membership -- typically an alumni accountant, a BOT member, or a third-party reviewer -- on at least an annual basis. The audit reviews chapter financial records, verifies that dues collected match deposits, and confirms that expenditures are consistent with the approved budget.

Even among True Merit chapters, fewer than half conduct a formal outside audit. This is an area where improvement opportunity exists across all chapters. Chapters that do conduct audits signal a level of financial discipline and transparency that protects both the organization and individual officers from personal liability.

Conversation

Ask when the chapter's finances were last reviewed by someone outside the active membership. If the answer is never or more than two years ago, the chapter is carrying financial risk that is not being managed.

Annual Member Dues
True Merit avg: $1,637/year Non-TM avg: $847

The total annual dues paid by an active initiated member. True Merit chapters charge nearly double the dues of non-TM chapters on average. Higher dues do not cause True Merit -- but they reflect something important: chapters that are doing more require more resources, and chapters whose members value their membership are willing to pay for it.

Dues that have not been adjusted in multiple years typically signal either a chapter that is not investing in programming and infrastructure, or a chapter whose leadership is conflict-averse about asking members to pay more.

Conversation

Ask when dues were last adjusted and what is included in the dues structure. Then ask what the chapter would do differently if it had 20 percent more revenue. The answer often reveals whether low dues reflect low ambition or genuine financial constraints.

Social Spend as % of Budget
True Merit avg: 24% Non-TM avg: 28%

The percentage of the chapter's operating budget (excluding housing) spent on social events and activities. Lower is better here. True Merit chapters spend a smaller share of their budget on social programming, which means a larger share is available for operations, scholarships, alumni programming, and other chapter functions.

A chapter directing 35 percent or more of its budget to social events is one whose spending priorities may not align with its stated values.

Conversation

Ask the treasurer what percentage of the annual budget goes to social events. If the number is significantly above 25 percent, ask what programming or infrastructure investment that spending is displacing.

Growth & Membership
Recruitment Committee Size
True Merit avg: 14 members Non-TM avg: 7

The number of initiated members serving on the Recruitment Committee. This is the single largest structural gap between True Merit and non-TM chapters in the growth category. True Merit chapters put twice as many men on their recruitment committee. A small committee concentrates recruiting responsibility in a handful of men whose personal networks determine who the chapter meets. A larger committee diversifies that network and creates broader chapter ownership of the recruitment outcome.

Conversation

Ask how many members are on the Recruitment Committee and what they are expected to do. If the committee is fewer than eight men or if its role is primarily administrative, the chapter is under-investing in its primary growth function.

Scholarship Program
True Merit chapters: 83% Non-TM: 53%

The chapter has a structured scholarship program -- not just a standard that members must maintain a minimum GPA, but an active program that includes resources for members who are struggling, recognition for members who excel, and defined processes for academic support. True Merit chapters are 30 points more likely to have this than non-TM chapters.

Conversation

Ask the president to describe the scholarship program -- what it includes, who runs it, and what happens when a member falls below the standard. A chapter that can describe only the minimum GPA requirement does not have a scholarship program. It has a rule.

Provides Tutors to Members
True Merit chapters: 37% Non-TM: 17%

The chapter pays for or arranges tutoring services for initiated members who need academic support. This moves the chapter's academic commitment from aspirational to operational -- it is not just encouraging members to succeed academically but actively resourcing their ability to do so. While fewer than half of True Merit chapters offer tutoring, the rate is more than double that of non-TM chapters.

Conversation

Ask whether tutoring is available to members who need it, how it is funded, and how members access it. A chapter that says tutoring is available but cannot describe the mechanism for accessing it typically does not have an active program.

Members Apply for ATO Foundation Scholarships
True Merit chapters: 52% Non-TM: 37%

Chapter members are actively encouraged to apply for scholarships available through the ATO Foundation, and the chapter has members who have applied in the current academic year. This reflects both the chapter's connection to the Foundation and whether chapter leadership actively promotes scholarship opportunities. A chapter whose members are not applying is leaving money on the table and missing an opportunity to deepen members' connection to the broader ATO organization.

Conversation

Ask whether the scholarship chairman promoted Foundation scholarships this year and how many members applied. If the answer is zero or uncertain, the chapter is not maintaining an active connection to Foundation resources.

Pledge Education Period Length
True Merit avg: 8 weeks Non-TM avg: 8 weeks

The number of weeks in the chapter's formal new member education program. True Merit chapters average eight weeks of structured pledge education. Chapters that compress this significantly -- running five- or six-week programs -- are shortening the period during which new members develop their understanding of ATO's history, values, and ritual before initiation.

The gap between True Merit and non-TM chapters on this indicator is modest overall, but among high-scoring chapters that do not win True Merit, a shorter pledge education period is a recurring pattern.

Conversation

Ask how many weeks the current pledge education program runs and what is covered each week. A chapter that has shortened its program due to calendar pressure or retention concerns is making a trade that affects the quality of the initiate experience.

Campus & External
Members in Student Government
True Merit chapters: 65% Non-TM: 33%

At least one chapter member holds a position in campus student government. This is the largest gap in the Campus and NHQ section -- TM chapters are twice as likely to have members in student government. Student government involvement signals that the chapter is producing members who are visible leaders on campus, not just within the Greek system.

Chapters with members in student government tend to be better-networked with campus administration, more aware of campus policy, and more likely to be seen as contributors to campus life rather than recipients of it. That reputation has downstream effects on recruitment, institutional support, and how the chapter is treated in difficult situations.

Conversation

Ask which members currently hold student government positions and whether the chapter actively encourages members to run. A chapter that has never had a member in student government should reflect on whether it is cultivating men who aspire to broad campus leadership.

Ten or More Service Events Per Year
True Merit chapters: 46% Non-TM: 19%

The chapter organizes and participates in more than ten service events over the course of the academic year. The 10-event threshold separates chapters that treat service as a regular chapter function from those that do one or two events per semester to meet a minimum expectation. True Merit chapters clear this bar at more than twice the rate of non-TM chapters.

High-frequency service programming requires an active committee, consistent scheduling, and member participation beyond a small core. A chapter doing ten or more events has made service a genuine chapter identity element, not a compliance checkbox.

Conversation

Ask how many service events the chapter has completed in the past twelve months and what events are planned for the rest of the year. A chapter that cannot list events by name has not treated service as a tracked chapter function.

Alumni Newsletter Semi-Annually
True Merit chapters: 75% Non-TM: 64%

The chapter produces and distributes a chapter newsletter to its alumni at least twice per academic year. The newsletter keeps alumni informed of chapter activities, connected to the current membership, and aware of opportunities to engage. It is one of the lowest-cost, highest-leverage alumni engagement tools available to any chapter.

Conversation

Ask when the most recent newsletter was sent, how many alumni it reached, and what was included. A chapter that has not sent a newsletter in more than six months has let the most basic alumni communication lapse.

Thomas Arkle Clark Nominee Submitted
True Merit chapters: 85% Non-TM: 65%

The chapter has submitted a nomination for the Thomas Arkle Clark Award, ATO's highest individual recognition for an initiated member. Submitting a nomination requires that the chapter has identified a member who embodies the values ATO recognizes at the highest level, documented that member's contributions, and engaged with the formal NHQ recognition process.

True Merit chapters are 20 points more likely to submit nominations. Submitting nominations is also a retention tool -- men who know they are in an organization that formally recognizes excellence are more likely to aspire to it.

Conversation

Ask whether the chapter submitted a Thomas Arkle Clark nomination this year and who was nominated. A chapter that has eligible men but did not submit is leaving a recognition opportunity unused.

Zone 3
Chapter Failure Metrics
These indicators predict closure. When anything here is flagged, the response is staff action -- not chapter coaching.

These indicators predict chapter closure. They are not primarily about whether a chapter is excellent -- they are about whether it is still functioning as an ATO chapter and maintaining the minimum institutional connections that keep it viable. When indicators in this zone are flagged, the appropriate response is staff action, not chapter coaching. An orange indicator here means someone from NHQ needs to make contact with the chapter this week.

Members Are Involved in Other Campus Organizations
Closing chapters: 80% Open chapters: 99%

Chapter members hold membership in at least one other campus organization beyond the fraternity. When this drops, it typically means the chapter has become socially isolated -- its members' entire campus social life runs through the fraternity, and the chapter has no outward connections to campus life through its individual members.

This is among the most consistent predictors of closure in the dataset. Nearly every open chapter passes this check. Chapters that close are doing so from a condition of isolation that typically built over multiple semesters before becoming visible in other data.

When it is orange

This requires a direct conversation with chapter leadership about whether members are engaged on campus beyond the fraternity. A chapter whose entire social ecosystem is internal is at serious risk of the kind of rapid membership decline that precedes closure.

Budget Prepared Each Term
Closing chapters: 76% Open chapters: 95%

The chapter prepares a formal budget at the start of each semester. This is distinct from having a treasurer -- it means the chapter is producing a documented spending plan, presenting it to chapter leadership, and operating against a defined financial framework each term.

A chapter without a current budget is operating financially without structure. It cannot track overspending, cannot make informed decisions about resource allocation, and cannot provide the BOT with meaningful financial reports. The absence of a term budget is one of the earliest observable signs of organizational deterioration.

When it is orange

Ask the chapter treasurer to share the current semester budget. If no budget exists or it has not been approved by the chapter, this is a priority action item. NHQ staff should follow up within two weeks to confirm a budget has been produced.

ATO Alumnus Performs DA Part at Initiation
Closing chapters: 56% Open chapters: 73%

An initiated ATO alumnus -- not just any alumni guest -- performs the Distinguished Alumnus part of the initiation ceremony. This requires that the chapter has an alumnus who is engaged enough to attend and prepared enough to perform a formal ritual role. It is both a ritual integrity indicator and a signal of alumni relationship depth.

When this fails, it usually means the chapter does not have alumni relationships deep enough to call on for this specific commitment. That is a leading indicator of broader alumni disengagement that typically predates closure.

When it is orange

Ask who performed the DA part at the most recent initiation. If no ATO alumnus was available or willing, ask when the chapter last had meaningful contact with its alumni network and what the plan is to rebuild those relationships.

Member Collection Program in Use
Closing chapters: 68% Open chapters: 91%

The chapter uses a formal collections process for members who are delinquent in dues payments -- a written policy with defined timelines, escalation steps, and consequences. Without a functioning collection program, the chapter is dependent on voluntary compliance for its operating revenue, which typically means a growing gap between dues billed and dues collected as the chapter struggles.

Financial deterioration and membership decline tend to reinforce each other: smaller chapters collect less revenue, which reduces their ability to program, which makes membership less attractive, which reduces the chapter further.

When it is orange

Ask the treasurer what happens when a member does not pay dues by the deadline. If the answer is informal or inconsistent, the chapter is not managing its revenue function. Ask about the current amount of outstanding dues and how long the oldest balance has been outstanding.

Annual Report Submitted to NHQ
Closing chapters: 67% Open chapters: 94%

The chapter has submitted its annual report and awards application to NHQ on schedule. The annual report is the primary formal channel by which the chapter documents its activities, reports its compliance with NHQ standards, and establishes its eligibility for award recognition. A chapter that has not submitted is either unable to document its year's work or has disengaged from NHQ processes entirely.

Missing the annual report is not a paperwork problem -- it is a symptom of a chapter that has lost its connection to the national organization.

When it is orange

Confirm with NHQ records whether the annual report has been received. If not, contact the chapter president directly to determine whether the chapter intends to submit and what support is needed. This should not be treated as a routine reminder.

H&S Compliance Form Submitted to NHQ
Closing chapters: 73% Open chapters: 96%

The chapter has submitted its required Health and Safety compliance form to NHQ on schedule. This form documents the chapter's completion of required health and safety education and its compliance with ATO's risk management standards. Non-submission creates legal and insurance exposure for both the chapter and the national organization.

Chapters that close submit this form at a 23-point lower rate than chapters that remain open. Non-submission is rarely accidental -- it reflects a chapter that is either not completing the required education or has stopped engaging with NHQ administrative requirements.

When it is orange

This requires immediate follow-up from NHQ staff. Confirm whether the chapter completed the required H&S education. Non-submission that persists beyond the deadline should escalate to the Director of Health and Safety.

Minutes Forwarded to NHQ
Closing chapters: 3% Open chapters: 33%

Chapter meeting minutes are forwarded to NHQ on a regular basis. This is the sharpest single divider between closing and open chapters in the entire failure metrics section -- closing chapters almost never do this. Minutes are the most basic form of NHQ visibility into chapter governance, and chapters that have stopped sending them have effectively gone dark.

A "Partial" value on this indicator -- reflecting inconsistent submission across the two survey terms -- is a meaningful flag. One term yes, one term no is a chapter whose connection to NHQ is inconsistent and declining.

When it is orange

Ask the chapter secretary when minutes were last forwarded to NHQ and whether this is a current practice. If minutes have not been sent in more than one semester, NHQ staff should request the most recent minutes directly.

Minutes Forwarded to BOT
Closing chapters: 16% Open chapters: 45%

Chapter meeting minutes are forwarded to the Board of Trustees on a regular basis. This is how the BOT stays informed of chapter business between meetings -- without minutes, the BOT is relying entirely on the chapter president's account of events, which limits its ability to provide meaningful oversight.

Only 16 percent of chapters that go on to close are forwarding minutes to their BOT. The BOT cannot govern what it cannot see.

When it is orange

Ask the chapter president when the BOT last received meeting minutes and whether this is a standing practice. If not, ask the BOT chair whether they are receiving information from the chapter and through what channel.

Newly Initiated Members Have Voting Rights
Closing chapters: 80% Open chapters: 97%

Newly initiated members are granted full voting rights at chapter meetings. When this is not happening, it typically signals either a chapter that has created an informal tiered membership system or a chapter where bylaws are not being followed. Either condition is a governance problem.

Denying voting rights to new initiates reduces their sense of ownership in the chapter, creates an insider-outsider dynamic that undermines brotherhood, and is associated with chapters in organizational decline.

When it is orange

Ask when newly initiated brothers receive voting rights and whether any current members have been initiated but are not yet voting. If new initiates are not voting immediately, ask what the basis for that practice is and whether the bylaws support it.

H&S Violation in the Last 18 Months
Closing chapters: 12% Open chapters: 14%

The chapter has had a formal Health and Safety policy violation recorded by ATO within the past eighteen months. This is a red flag indicator -- its presence signals that a violation is on record, with implications for the chapter's standing, its H&S rate, and the conversation NHQ staff should be having with chapter leadership.

Note that the rate difference between closing and open chapters on this indicator is small -- violations occur across chapter types. What matters is whether the chapter has addressed the underlying conditions and changed the behaviors that led to it.

When it is orange

Ask chapter leadership what happened, what corrective action was taken, and what has changed since the violation. A chapter that describes the violation minimally or cannot name specific changes made is a chapter that has not learned from it.

Campus Policy Violation in the Last 18 Months
Closing chapters: 4% Open chapters: 17%

The chapter has received a formal campus policy violation from the university within the past eighteen months. Campus violations damage the chapter's relationship with the institution and can restrict chapter activities, affect housing status, or place the chapter on probation with the IFC or the university's Greek Life office.

The rate difference here is meaningful in an unexpected direction: closing chapters have fewer campus violations on record than open chapters. This is because chapters that are declining often have fewer events and lower visibility. The absence of violations is not a sign of health -- it may be a sign of inactivity.

When it is orange

Ask chapter leadership what the violation involved, what the university's response was, and what the chapter's current standing is with the institution's Greek Life office. Confirm the chapter has completed any required sanctions or educational programming.

Property or Hotel Damage
Closing chapters: 13% Open chapters: 2%

The chapter has been responsible for property or hotel damage within the past twelve months. This indicator has the sharpest rate difference in the failure metrics section: closing chapters are more than six times as likely to have a property damage incident on record. Property damage incidents typically reflect events that were poorly managed, inadequately supervised, or that involved behavior inconsistent with ATO's risk management standards.

Beyond the financial cost, these incidents create institutional liability, damage the chapter's reputation on campus and in the community, and often indicate broader risk management failures that extend beyond the specific incident.

When it is orange

This requires an immediate and direct conversation with chapter leadership. Ask what happened, who was involved, how the damage was addressed financially, and what changes have been made to event management and supervision practices. This should be escalated to the Director of Health and Safety regardless of whether the chapter believes the incident was resolved.
