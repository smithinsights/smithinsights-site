Overview

This report analyzes survey responses from 94 former members who resigned from Alpha Tau Omega after initiation. All 94 respondents were matched by 18-character Salesforce ID to the member cohort database and confirmed as Former Members. A duplicate submission was identified and removed (earlier response retained). Survey responses are rated on a five-point scale (1 = did not influence, 5 = was the primary reason I left). Unlike the depledge survey, no N/A option existed - skipped items are treated as missing and excluded from means.

The 94 respondents represent a 2.5% response rate against the 3,737 Former Members coded as Resigned in the cohort database. Survey collection occurred in two waves using identical 23-item instruments. Duration was calculated from Initiation Date to Resignation Date using cohort records, with Current Status Begin Date used as fallback where Resignation Date was null.

Key Finding

Resignation has two distinct failure modes with different causes and different intervention needs. Year 1 resigners leave for financial and logistical reasons while still connected - and 22% regret it. Year 2 resigners leave because the chapter failed them on belonging, conflict, and worth - and 95% do not regret it. The recoverable population and the culture-failure population require entirely different responses.

Factor Structure: Four Themes

An exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the 55 complete-case respondents (all 23 items answered). Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin = 0.663 (mediocre, adequate for direction-finding at this sample size). Bartlett's test p < .001. Four factors retained by Kaiser criterion, explaining 43.2% of total variance. Varimax rotation. Each factor is a cluster of items that co-occur - men who cited one item in a cluster tended to cite others in the same cluster.

Structural Difference from Depledge

In the depledge survey (n=591), hazing, bullying, and alcohol culture formed a distinct Toxic Environment factor separate from belonging and values. In this resignation data, those same items load onto the same factor as Belonging, Worth, and Values. Initiated members do not separate toxic behavior from belonging failure. The fraternity's safety failures and its social failures are experienced as one unified disillusionment after initiation.

Among initiated members who resigned, they collapse into the same construct as Belonging and Values, meaning initiated members do not separate toxic behavior from belonging failure - they experience the chapter's social environment and its safety failures as one unified disillusionment.

Academic pressure, major or time-demand conflicts, and in some cases leaving school entirely cluster together. These are forces external to the fraternity competing with membership for time and energy. No statistically significant variation across lifecycle buckets, though the item-level means are slightly elevated in Year 1 when new members are still establishing their academic footing.

Two financial items load cleanly together: dues funded by parents and accumulated debt burdens. This cluster reflects members whose financial connection to the fraternity was through others or had compounded over time. Distinct from self-funded financial pressure, which correlates with the Culture and Fit cluster rather than this one.

Coach program dissatisfaction and significant organizational change load together weakly (alpha = 0.392). This factor is below the reliability threshold for confident interpretation. Conflict cross-loads between F1 and F4; Coach program cross-loaded onto the Toxic Environment factor in the depledge data. Treat these items individually rather than as a construct.
Resignation Lifecycle

Members were bucketed into four lifecycle windows based on months from Initiation Date to resignation. Items with statistically significant variation across buckets (Kruskal-Wallis, p < 0.05): Worth, Belonging, Conflict, Experience gap, Mental health, Time, and Values. Post-hoc pairwise tests confirm Year 2 as the peak of cultural disillusionment, with Worth (4.05), Conflict, Experience gap, and Values all significantly higher than Year 3 (p < 0.05). Mental health peaks uniquely in Year 2 (p < 0.01 vs. both adjacent buckets).

Y1
Year 1 (0-12 mo)
n = 36 respondents  ·  22% regret (n=27)

Financial cost and early disillusionment drive Year 1 resignations. Men paying dues out of pocket for the first time, an experience that has not yet matched the recruitment pitch, and chapter ties not yet deeply formed. Time demands add pressure. 22% of Year 1 resigners express regret, the highest rate of any bucket, and not one regretter was offered a genuine financial solution or alternative before resigning. This is the highest-yield intervention window in the dataset.

Regret Analysis

75 respondents answered the regret question. 86.7% expressed no regret; 13.3% did. Two items reached statistical significance in Mann-Whitney U tests: Belonging (p=0.008) and Academics/major-time (p=0.028). Bullying (p=0.076) and Rules (p=0.078) approached significance. The regret split reveals two fundamentally different resignation profiles.

Yes, I regret resigning
13.3%  ·  n=10
Median tenure: 10.8 months from initiation  ·  Belonging avg: 1.60  ·  Financial avg: 3.40

These men left for external, logistical reasons - cost above all else - while still connected to the chapter. Their Belonging score is low (1.60), meaning they did not resign because they felt they did not belong. They felt they did belong, and circumstances forced them out. Not one regretter was offered a genuine solution before departing (8 No, 2 Sort of). This group is recoverable. They left a chapter they wanted to stay in, and no one tried to keep them.

No, I do not regret resigning
86.7%  ·  n=64
Median tenure: 17.7 months from initiation  ·  Belonging avg: 2.85  ·  Worth avg: 3.36

Non-regretters rated Belonging significantly higher (2.85 vs. 1.60, p=0.008), as well as Worth, Experience gap, Values, and Bullying. These men left because the chapter failed to meet expectations, failed to treat them well, or failed to align with their values. They made a deliberate decision and are at peace with it. Longer tenure correlates with lower regret - men who stayed longer had more time to confirm their assessment before acting.

Intervention: Were Solutions Offered?

The solutions question was answered by 76 respondents. The intervention rate is substantially lower than in the depledge analysis, where approximately half of respondents were offered something. Chapters are less likely to intervene with initiated members who signal departure than with prospective members who withdraw from pledging.

Intervention Window

Year 1 resigners represent 38% of respondents, carry a 22% regret rate, and cite financial pressure as their primary driver. None of the 10 men who expressed regret received a genuine retention offer before resigning. A structured financial relief process - dues deferral, payment plans, or chapter emergency funds - applied to Year 1 members signaling financial hardship would address the dataset's highest-yield retention opportunity.

Methodology Notes
  • Sample: 94 respondents matched to cohort as Former Members. Two survey waves with identical 23-item instruments. One duplicate submission removed (earlier response retained).
  • Factor analysis: Complete cases only (n=55). KMO = 0.663 (mediocre, adequate for direction-finding). Four factors retained by Kaiser criterion, varimax rotation. F4 alpha = 0.392 - interpret with caution.
  • Duration: Initiation Date to Resignation Date using cohort records. Current Status Begin Date used as endpoint where Resignation Date was null (17 of 95 records).
  • Regret analysis: Mann-Whitney U tests, two-sided. n=74 answered. Significance at p<0.05; marginal at p<0.10 noted.
  • Influence scoring: Did not influence=1, Had a small influence=2, Somewhat influenced=3, Had a significant influence=4, Was the primary reason I left=5. No N/A option on this instrument. Skipped items excluded from means and chart bars.
  • Population context: 3,737 Former Members coded as Resigned in the database. Survey response rate: 2.5%. Results reflect self-selected respondents and should not be generalized to all resignations.
