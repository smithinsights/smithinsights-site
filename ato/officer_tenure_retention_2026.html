Officer Tenure and Membership Retention

June 2026  ·  Director of Systems  ·  National Fraternity

This analysis asks whether holding an officer position changes whether an undergraduate member finishes his membership as an alumnus, whether the specific role matters, and whether chapter size changes the answer. It draws on the full initiated membership cohort from 2007 through 2026, joined to the complete officer service record, and it separates a member's eventual departure into the two routes that actually differ: voluntary resignation and for-cause expulsion.

Reading the effect honestly

A raw comparison of members who ever held a role against those who never did shows a 12.5 point retention gap. Most of that gap is an artifact. To be elected to a role, a member first has to survive long enough to be eligible, so officers are pre-selected for having already stayed. The comparison only becomes fair once it is anchored to a fixed point in every member's timeline.

This analysis uses a 12-month landmark. Each member is classified by whether he had taken a role within his first year, and his outcome is measured only from that point forward, among members who were all still active at the landmark. Two further contaminants were removed: members whose departure was caused by a chapter closure rather than their own choice, and a set of administrative records that had been bulk-reclassified to former-member status on database sweep dates.

Cleaning moves the baseline more than it moves the officer effect. Once involuntary departures are out, 93.5 percent of members who survive their first year reach alumnus regardless of whether they hold a role. The first year is where nearly all attrition happens. Leadership adds to a baseline that is already high.

Holding a role matters, modestly

Among members who survived to the 12-month landmark, those who had taken a role reach alumnus at 95.6 percent against 93.5 percent for those who had not. Adjusted for cohort era and chapter size, the odds ratio is 1.72 and the difference is statistically reliable. In plain terms the lift is about two points on top of a high baseline. The effect is real and worth acting on, and it is smaller than the unadjusted numbers suggest.

The role gradient runs through the executive seats

Not every title carries the same signal. Measured at a 24-month landmark, which gives the senior roles a fair chance to appear since members reach them later in their tenure, the retention effect concentrates in the executive officers. President, Vice President, Treasurer, and Chaplain, together with Alumni Relations, all predict reaching alumnus at a reliable level. The committee chairs do not.

Splitting departures: resignation and expulsion behave differently

Combining every kind of departure into one outcome hides the most useful finding. When the former-member group is split into voluntary resignation and for-cause expulsion, holding a role protects against both, and the protection is proportionally larger for expulsion. Against the alternative of reaching alumnus, officers carry a resignation relative risk of 0.64 and an expulsion relative risk of 0.49. Resignation is where the volume sits, so resignation is where the practical leverage is.

The roles that prevent resignation are not the same roles that produce alumni. Reaching graduation tracks with the executive seats. Resistance to resignation tracks with the relational roles. Chaplain is the single strongest resignation deterrent, which fits its place at the center of the ritual and the values of the National Fraternity. Alumni Relations ties a member to the wider organization. Recruitment Chairman, which never predicted graduation, sharply reduces resignation: the member who invests in bringing others in does not walk away.

Expulsion is rare once closures and disciplinary mass actions are removed, with 196 cases in the cleaned data. The signal still points the same direction. Members in the executive tier are expelled for cause at 0.29 percent against 0.64 percent for everyone else, roughly half the rate. The pool is small, so treat this as supporting evidence rather than a precise estimate, but the members who take responsible roles are less often the ones removed.

The protective effect grows with chapter size

Chapter size changes how much a role matters, and the resignation data shows it most clearly. Officers resign at a low and steady rate regardless of how large their chapter is. Non-officers resign more as the chapter grows. In a small chapter there is almost no difference between the two. In a large chapter a non-officer is nearly twice as likely to resign as an officer.

The mechanism is anonymity. In a small chapter every member is visible and engaged, and almost no one resigns whether or not he holds a title. As a chapter grows, a member without a defined role can drift, and a role is what anchors him. The practical reading is direct: the larger the chapter, the more it matters to put members into roles. One related point of precision, the size of the chapter at the moment a member happens to take a role has no bearing on his own retention. It is the ongoing size context that moves the gap.

Roles do not change when a member graduates

Officers and non-officers reach alumnus on essentially the same timeline, a median of 3.13 years against 3.14 years from initiation. Holding a role is associated with being more likely to finish, not with finishing sooner or later. There is no sign of leadership burnout pulling members out early, and no sign of officers lingering.

Data quality finding

A set of 2,084 members had been bulk-reclassified to former-member status on a handful of database sweep dates. A single date, 25 January 2019, accounts for simultaneous reclassifications across 146 chapters. Leaving these records in nearly doubles the apparent officer effect, because the swept members are disproportionately non-officers. Any retention or attrition work built on the cohort sheet should filter these sweep dates first. The same filter belongs in the depledge and resignation survey pipelines.

What to do with this

1
Push role placement hardest in large chaptersOfficer status closes nearly the entire resignation gap in large chapters and almost none of it in small ones. Chapters above the size median should treat getting every member into a defined role as a retention lever, not a nicety.
2
Separate the goal before choosing the roleIf the aim is graduation rates, the executive seats carry the signal. If the aim is stopping voluntary departure, the relational roles carry it. Chaplain, Alumni Relations, Recruitment, and Secretary are the resignation anchors and should be filled deliberately, not left as afterthoughts.
3
Treat the Chaplain role as a retention positionChaplain is the strongest single deterrent against resignation in the data. Chapters that leave it weak or unfilled are giving up the role most tied to keeping members.
4
Read the headline as the first year, then the roleMost attrition happens before the 12-month mark. The officer effect is a second-order lift on members who already survived that window. First-year experience and role placement are sequential priorities, not competing ones.
5
Apply the sweep-date filter across the analytics stackBuild the 25 January 2019 and related sweep-date exclusions into every recurring pipeline that reads member status, so attrition figures are not inflated by administrative reclassification.
Method note

Population. Initiated members in the 2007 to 2026 cohort. Pre-charter Nexus group members from the pre-charter period are excluded; legitimate prior Open-chapter brothers of those same chapters are retained. Whether Nexus removal is handled this way or by removing all affected chapters wholesale does not change any conclusion.

Landmark. Each member is held to a fixed point after initiation, 12 months for the core analysis and 24 months for the role gradient. Only members still active at the landmark enter the analysis, and outcomes are measured from the landmark forward. Current undergraduates whose outcome is not yet known are censored out.

Cleaning. Removed from the modeling sample: 910 members whose departure fell within a chapter closure window, 509 post-restart Nexus-era members, and 2,084 members bulk-reclassified on administrative sweep dates. Closures were detected as chapter-specific mass departures after which the chapter ceased to initiate.

Models. Logistic regression for alumnus attainment and for each departure route, multinomial logistic for the competing-risk split, all adjusted for cohort era and chapter-size tier. The per-role resignation model is fit on sparse cells; the four protective roles named are those that clear significance. Departure-type composition rests on the roughly 1,360 former members carrying a coded departure reason; 629 carry none and are held out of the split.

Caveat. The landmark removes the survivorship artifact but not selection. Officers are elected volunteers, so the effect mixes a genuine engagement contribution with the reality that already-committed members are the ones who run for and win these roles.
