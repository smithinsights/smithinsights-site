FEA 2026 -- Session Summary for Wynn

May 28, 2026  ·  Steve Whitby, Cabin  ·  General Session + COO Deep Dive
Session 1  ·  9:45 - 10:45am
From Experimentation to Infrastructure: Leading with Intention in an AI-Enabled Organization
Steve Whitby is a partner at Cabin, an AI transformation consultancy based in Charlotte. He spent a decade at Pi Kappa Phi before moving into technology consulting, which gave him real credibility in this room. He understands how national fraternities operate -- volunteer structures, campus politics, staff capacity constraints -- and was not speaking from a generic enterprise frame. The session was substantive and well-structured.
AI Is Not One Category

Whitby opened by calling "let's talk about AI" the wrong question -- too broad to answer and too vague to produce action. AI is a wide taxonomy of distinct technologies, each with different governance implications. He flagged four as the priority direction: agents, industry-specific AI (including MCP), search and knowledge retrieval, and data analysis and BI. These are also where most of ATO's current work lives.

Governance Implication

A policy treating all AI as a single category will be too blunt to be effective. Policy needs to be use-case and data-sensitivity based -- not tool-name based -- because tool lists become outdated almost immediately. Likewise, embedded AI features already appearing inside platforms staff use daily (Salesforce, ChapterBuilder, the database) may already be in use with no policy awareness at all.

Three Domains of AI

Whitby organized organizational AI activity into three domains, each requiring different governance treatment:

Category 1
The Way We Do Our Work
Individual staff productivity -- drafting, summarization, workflow assistance, research support.
Category 2
The Way We Run Our Organization
Operational systems, internal decision support, process automation, and reporting workflows.
Category 3
What We Offer to Members and Partners
Member-facing products, services, and experiences. The highest-risk domain and the least governed in most associations.

Category 3 carries the most organizational risk. Anything touching undergraduate members -- particularly around conduct, health, or recruitment -- warrants explicit human oversight policy before AI is anywhere in the loop.

The 2x2 Framework: Measuring AI Activity

Whitby introduced a framework for mapping AI activity across two dimensions: automation/acceleration (how much AI removes humans from the loop) and augmentation (how much AI extends human capability). His recommended near-term focus: Equalizer and Efficiency Engine. Multiplier (agents) requires mature infrastructure most organizations have not yet built.

Every organization in 2026 should be operating in On Ramp at minimum. The honest state for most attendees: still in informal, individual experimentation with no organizational posture. ATO is operating across all four quadrants depending on the function and the person -- which is both an asset and a governance gap.

Affordance: Why Policy Alone Does Not Work

Affordance describes what a tool communicates it wants you to do with it. AI tools like Claude and ChatGPT have low affordance -- the blank text box does not tell staff what to do, how to do it well, or what is off limits. Written policy that no one reads will not change behavior.

Governance Principle

Deploy AI the way you hire a staff member. Define the role, set expectations, establish what decisions it can make independently vs. which require human review, and build in evaluation. Governance has to be built into the workflow itself -- structured prompts, defined use cases, and clear entry points that communicate correct use.

Where to Start: Three Questions

Before any AI initiative, organizations should answer these three questions. These are the right frame for the ATO technology summit in June -- they replace "what do we do about AI" with something answerable.

Self
Personal Adoption
"What is one repetitive job I want to hire AI for?" Individual capability and adoption come first.
Organization
Team Posture
"Do I have a clear stance on how my team uses AI?" A documented posture -- not just opinions -- that actually shapes behavior.
Mission
Member Experience
"What is a touchpoint in the member journey where we've had to compromise on quality or consistency because of capacity?" This is the right frame for grounding AI adoption in purpose.

He also offered a critical framing: AI will not do the foundational organizational work for you. Organizations that skip clean data, documented processes, and defined governance will hit a ceiling regardless of what tools they adopt.

Session 2  ·  11:00am - 12:00pm
Don't Automate the Mess: A Framework for Meaningful AI Experimentation in Ops and Programs
The Core Principle

Whitby opened with a direct statement: "AI won't fix a broken process. It will just break it faster." Organizations are automating broken workflows and accelerating the dysfunction. The session introduced a practical framework for identifying the right problems before applying AI to them.

He distinguished between a project (deliverables, timelines, stakeholders) and an experiment (one question it is trying to answer). Most organizations are running AI projects when they should be running AI experiments -- which means they are over-committing resources and under-learning.

Three Diagnostic Questions

Before writing a use case, Whitby asked three questions to surface the right problems:

Question 1
What takes longer than it should?
Efficiency Engine candidates -- recurring, time-consuming, low-judgment tasks that have been normalized as "just how it works."
Question 2
What only works when the right person is in the seat?
The Equalizer opportunity -- knowledge or capability that is person-dependent and therefore organizationally fragile.
Question 3
What do you rebuild from scratch more than you should?
Anything rebuilt because it was never captured or systematized. AI won't fix this -- documentation and scoping discipline do.

He connected these back to the affordance concept from session one: manual steps embedded in workflows become invisible infrastructure. People stop seeing them as problems and start seeing them as process. Making them visible again is the precondition for AI to help.

The Use Case Framework

Whitby provided a five-sentence template for writing AI experiments. Every proposed use case at the technology summit should be expressed in this format before any tool discussion begins:

5-Sentence Use Case Template
1
We always have to [do something], and it takes [some amount] of our time or capacity.
2
I want to experiment with AI handling [some task] so that [some result I want happens].
3
We'll try the smallest version by [doing something specific] -- which looks like [describe an action you can actually do].
4
We'll know it's working when [this happens].
5
We'll stop if [this happens].

The "familiar vs. unfamiliar" distinction Whitby offered is useful for the first sentence: the right problems are ones where the workflow is familiar but the outcome is unpredictable, inconsistent, or slower than it should be. Sentence 5 is often skipped -- it should not be. A defined stop condition is what makes this an experiment rather than an unaccountable project.

Where ATO Stands

Relative to most of the room, ATO is ahead. The majority of attendees were at On Ramp or still in informal individual experimentation. ATO is already operating in all four quadrants depending on the function and the person.

The honest state: practice is outpacing policy. Staff are using AI in real workflows without a consistent documented stance. That is the actual risk. The opportunity is formalizing what already works and extending it intentionally.

Action Items for ATO

1
Answer the three starting questions before the technology summit in June Self (individual AI role), Organization (documented stance), Mission (capacity-constrained member touchpoints). These frame the summit rather than opening it with "what do we do about AI."
2
Use the five-sentence use case template as the summit exercise Every director fills in all five sentences for at least one use case before the meeting ends. No tool discussion until the use case is written. This gives governance something concrete to work with.
3
Establish a staff-wide On Ramp baseline Every staff member should be actively using at least one AI-assisted workflow. Uneven adoption is the current exposure -- some staff are deep in Efficiency Engine territory with no governance, others have not started.
4
Increase affordance for staff Don't just provide tool access. Build structured prompts, defined use cases, and workflow entry points that tell staff how to use AI well and where the limits are. Policy without affordance does not change behavior.
5
Map ATO workflows to the 2x2 matrix Plot current and target AI use by role and function. Identify which workflows belong in Efficiency Engine vs. Equalizer, and use that map to prioritize governance decisions and summit conversations.
6
Evaluate Basecamp 5 against Efficiency Engine use cases Whether it can support an ATO-wide operational calendar tied to the academic year. The communications version already exists -- the question is whether the model extends org-wide with AI assist.
7
Build use-case-based governance, not tool-name-based governance Organize policy around data sensitivity, domain (staff / org / member-facing), and quadrant placement. Tool lists are outdated before they are published.
