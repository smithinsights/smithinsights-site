Coaching the Men of ATO

June 2026  ·  Birkman Method  ·  Chapter Coaching
Membership Insight
What the Birkman data tells us about the men coming into our chapters, and how to coach them

The Birkman Method reads a man three ways. How he comes across to the people around him. What he needs from those people to do his best work. And how he behaves under stress when that need goes unmet. Most of the time the first two line up, and you can trust the surface to tell you what the man needs.

For the men entering ATO, those two readings have been pulling apart. The younger the member, the more his surface style can point you in the wrong direction about what he actually needs. A coach who responds to what he sees is increasingly responding to the wrong thing, and the chapter reads the fallout as an officer who checked out or got difficult. The real driver is usually a need that nobody read.

The shift in plain terms

This is not about today's members being weaker, softer, or harder to work with. The profiles are not worse. They are harder to read from the outside, because what a young man shows you and what he needs from you have moved further apart than they were even a decade ago.

Three of these gaps matter most for how we coach chapters and work with officers. In each one, the instinct a coach brings to the room is the response the surface invites, and it is the opposite of what the man needs.

Three shifts in how to coach
Shift One
The straightforward man needs more affirmation, not less
What you seeDirect, matter-of-fact, clear answers, seems at ease and able to take it straight.
What he needsGenuine respect, encouragement, criticism balanced with real acknowledgment, words chosen with care.

The typical young member presents as direct and unbothered. He gives you a clean answer and does not look thin-skinned, so the instinct is to be blunt back, skip the encouragement, and move straight to the correction. That instinct is increasingly wrong.

Underneath the direct style, his need for respect and affirmation has grown. When he gets blunt correction without it, he does not push back in the moment. He goes quiet, gets evasive, and pulls away. Coaches read that as disengagement. It is an unmet need for respect. Pair every correction with genuine acknowledgment, and be explicit that you respect his role and his work.

Shift Two
Recognize the man by name, not only the chapter
What you seeTeam-first language, talks about the brotherhood and the group, plays down his own part.
What he needsTo be recognized as an individual for what he personally did, by name.

Younger members talk in terms of the chapter and the group, so the instinct is to recognize the chapter as a unit. Group praise alone underfeeds them. The need for individual recognition has risen sharply, and it sits behind a collaborative surface that hides it.

The man who carried the recruitment push needs to hear his own name, not only that the chapter did well. Build individual acknowledgment into how you coach officers and how chapters hand out recognition. When you praise, name the person and the specific thing he did.

Shift Three
Give clear authority to the man who looks easygoing
What you seeAgreeable, low-key, less forceful than officers used to be. Looks flexible and easy to work with.
What he needsClear lines of authority, defined expectations, firm direction, room to debate issues openly.

Younger officers come across as more agreeable and less verbally forceful than officers did ten years ago. The instinct is to read that as low ownership, or to hand them loose, open-ended direction because they seem flexible. Both misread the man.

His need for clear structure and defined authority has risen. Vague direction and conflict-avoidant advising frustrate him. When that need goes unmet, the easygoing officer becomes argumentative or dug-in under stress, which catches everyone off guard. Give younger officers concrete expectations, clear authority, and a setting where issues get debated in the open.

The through-line
The one thing to carry into every chapter

Stress behavior in a chapter is mostly an unmet need, not a character flaw. The officer who goes evasive, the one who will not take feedback, the one who suddenly digs in, is usually a man whose need went unread because his surface style pointed the other way. Coach to the need, not to the presentation.

Whether this reflects a true generational change or the stage of life these men are in, the coaching response is the same. Read past the surface, and respond to what the man needs.

What this changes in the room

On a chapter visit or a one-on-one with an officer, these are the habits to bring and the habits to coach into advisors and older members, who interact with these men daily and whose instincts need recalibrating most.

  • Lead with genuine respect and acknowledgment before correction, even with men who look like they can take it straight.
  • Name individuals when you recognize work. Tie the praise to the specific thing the person did, not only to the chapter.
  • Give officers defined authority and concrete expectations rather than open-ended encouragement.
  • When an officer goes quiet, evasive, or suddenly argumentative, treat it as a signal of an unmet need. Ask what he needs before pushing harder.
  • Do not let a quiet, social, or easygoing surface tell you what the man needs. The surface and the need are further apart than they used to be.
