What Chapter Presidents Are Navigating

2026 ATO Congress  ·  President Linkage Session

At the 2026 ATO Congress, the National Fraternity board held a direct-linkage lunch with chapter presidents, following the Carver governance model. Each president answered five questions in writing before a board member facilitated the discussion. Ninety-three presidents responded, spanning every operating level: 26 True Merit, 11 Honorable Mention, 51 Not Considered, and 5 Nexus groups. This brief codes those written answers for the Leadership Consultants who work directly with chapters. The aim is practical: to sharpen how we prepare officers at the presidents retreat and how Leadership Consultants coach and onboard chapters this fall.

The Experience a Chapter President Navigates

Four patterns showed up across the responses regardless of chapter size or operating level. They are the shared reality every president is carrying, and the ground every coaching conversation this fall starts from.

1Disengagement is the common denominator

When presidents describe what their men struggle with, two-thirds land on some form of disengagement: apathy, a missing sense of why they joined, accountability that never lands, or comfort with the way things are. It is the single most common theme in the dataset, and it does not stay in one question. The same disengagement is what presidents call the hardest thing to navigate and the main thing holding them back. For the men in the room, the through-line is loneliness at the top. The president is frequently the only person holding the standard.

"We have a group who really want to achieve stuff and then a number who are there for a good time."
45 men, Honorable Mention chapter
2The exec-to-burnout loop

Disengagement does not stop at the general membership. When officers do not carry their share, the president absorbs the work, and then he runs out of gas. Officer underperformance, the inability to delegate, and personal burnout are one mechanism caught at three stages. This is the most actionable pattern in the data, because it does not require changing ninety-three chapters' worth of member attitudes. It points at officer development and role clarity, which the retreat can build directly.

"I can lead the train, build the cargo, but I need help building the tracks."
37 men, Not Considered chapter
"I am tired, and with the new Nexus strategies being implemented I don't know if I have the energy to push back on the guys that push back on me."
11 men, Nexus group
3The role was bigger than they pictured

Nearly half of presidents discovered the job was larger and different than they expected. They walked in picturing an external, outward-facing role and found the weight was internal, relational, and constant. The presidents who said the job matched expectations were almost entirely the ones who had served on an exec board before. That is a clean onboarding gap. Presidents are arriving without an accurate picture of the office, and the ones who had a picture got it from prior experience the others never had.

"I thought it would be much more like a VP than it is. It is actually much more like a chaplain, which I like. Much more personable and problem solving."
42 men, True Merit chapter
4They are leading into a headwind

The campus environment reads as hostile. The most common external influence presidents named was an administration or a council working against them, cited by 43 percent. Add the chapters whose reputation is dragged down by other fraternities' behavior, and the picture is a president squeezed from above by the university, sideways by a weak council, and by association with peers he does not control. The internal disengagement is real, and it is playing out against an external backdrop that offers little support.

"Limitations from university. Feeling like I'm on an island."
75 men, True Merit chapter
Discussion Prompts
  • Which of these four is each of your chapters leading with right now, and does our current support match it?
  • Where are we reading a burnout problem as a weak president?
  • Which incoming presidents have prior exec experience, and which are walking in without a picture of the job?
The Size Lens: Building Coaching Tracks

Size sorts the president's job more cleanly than any other variable, and it does it without ranking anyone. A 20-man president and a 150-man president are running different organizations, and everyone in the room already knows it. Held apart from operating level, size splits the work into two coaching tracks.

Size and operating level move together in this group. True Merit chapters average about 100 undergraduates and Not Considered chapters about 42, so a raw size pattern could be an operating-level pattern in disguise. The splits below were tested within a single operating level to rule that out. They hold on size alone.

Smaller chapters
The president is the operation
Roughly under 40 undergraduates. Delegation is close to impossible because there is no bench to hand work to. Manpower and recruitment are existential. The hardest thing these presidents name is keeping the operation running. Coaching centers on systems, recruitment, and protecting the president from doing every job himself.
Larger chapters
The president cannot know everyone
Roughly 60 undergraduates and up. There are enough hands to run the operation, so the problem flips to alignment and authority. Buy-in, cohesion, factions, and enforcing standards over a group too large to lead by relationship are the recurring struggles. Coaching centers on delegation structures, communication systems, and exercising authority at scale.

Within a single operating level, naming "managing the operation" as the hardest thing skews small, a median chapter size of 20 against 38. Naming "membership won't follow" as what holds a president back skews the other way, a median of 38 against 25. Same office, opposite problems, driven by size.

Discussion Prompts
  • Where is the size inflection point in your portfolio, the number where the job flips from running the operation to aligning the crowd?
  • What does a 20-man president need at the retreat that a 120-man president does not, and the reverse?
  • Are our onboarding materials written for one size and handed to all of them?
Field Diagnostic: Reading the Flavor of Disengagement
Internal · For Leadership Consultants · A Diagnostic, Not a Coaching Tier

This section is for the Leadership Consultants who sit with chapters. It is a read tool, and it is not meant to be announced to a chapter as a ranking. Disengagement shows up at nearly the same rate at every operating level. Roughly three-quarters of True Merit presidents and seven in ten Not Considered presidents named it. What changes with operating level is the flavor.

The Pattern

When presidents describe their members' struggles, complacency appears only among True Merit chapters, about one in four of them, and in none of the Not Considered or Honorable Mention chapters. Apathy runs flat across every level. Same disengagement, opposite root, opposite fix.

Lower-operating chapters
Apathy
The read is "my men do not care." The counter is purpose and accountability: a reason to show up and a standard that actually lands.
True Merit chapters
Complacency
The read is "we have already arrived." The counter is a higher bar and productive discomfort, because the risk at the top is standing still.

The practical move is to name the flavor before prescribing. Telling a complacent chapter to find its why, or an apathetic chapter to raise the bar, is the right medicine for the wrong patient. Honorable Mention (11 presidents) and Nexus (5 groups) are too small to cut with confidence and are read descriptively only.

Discussion Prompts
  • When you diagnose a chapter, which flavor are you seeing, and does your coaching language match it?
  • Where have we prescribed "find your why" to a chapter that needed a higher bar, or the reverse?
There Is No President Archetype

We tested whether presidents sort into types. Using each president's full set of codes, we ran a person-level correspondence analysis and clustering, first on the 66 specific codes and then on the grouped themes. Neither level produced stable archetypes. The dominant challenges, disengagement, the scope of the job, exercising authority, and institutional hostility, are so widely shared that they cannot separate one president from another. The only differentiation comes from a few uncommon concerns that mark small handfuls of presidents rather than balanced groups.

The Result

No president archetype emerged at either the specific-code or the grouped-theme level. The core of the job is common ground. That is an argument for teaching the shared load well rather than sorting presidents by personality or chapter.

A few themes do reliably travel together from one president to the next. These are worth carrying into a coaching conversation as "when you see this, look for that" pairings.

Themes That Travel Together
  • A president fighting the administration or council is usually also the one who struggles most to motivate the chapter.
  • Member disengagement rarely stops at the general membership. In the same chapters, the exec board is not executing either.
  • Where cliques, ego, and age divides appear, the president also feels he cannot move the group.
  • Money pressure and members under personal strain show up together, the strapped-chapter signature.
  • The president who frames his role as a mentor is the one most tuned in to his members' wellbeing.
Implications for the Retreat and Onboarding

Several findings convert directly into retreat and onboarding design. They are offered here as questions for planning rather than a fixed program, since the Leadership Consultants closest to chapters will build the interventions.

Questions to Take Into Retreat Planning

1
The preparation gapPresidents without prior exec experience were blindsided by the scope of the job. What would it take to give every incoming president an accurate picture of the office before day one?
2
The exec benchThe burnout loop is an officer-development problem more than a president problem. How much of the retreat should build the full exec board rather than the president alone?
3
Size tracksThe job differs most by size. Where would we split the room, and what does each track need that the other does not?
4
The disengagement flavorLeadership Consultants are diagnosing apathy and complacency in different chapters. How do we equip them to name the flavor and match the intervention?
5
The external headwindPresidents feel unsupported by administrations and councils. What can the National Fraternity carry for them that they cannot carry alone?
Method

The data are the written answers of 93 chapter presidents to five questions at the 2026 ATO Congress linkage session. Responses span all operating levels: 26 True Merit, 11 Honorable Mention, 51 Not Considered, and 5 Nexus groups. Answers were coded inductively, one question at a time, allowing more than one theme per answer where the text supported it. Specific codes were then grouped into the parent themes reported above. Frequencies are the share of coded answers that carried a theme, so a question's themes can sum to more than 100 percent. Operating-level and size effects were tested with Fisher exact and Mann-Whitney tests, and the complacency pattern reaches p = 0.004. Person-level structure was examined with correspondence analysis and k-means clustering on both the specific codes and the grouped themes, and no stable president archetype emerged. A second coder independently re-coded a sample of responses from the codebooks alone as a reliability check, and the two codings closely agreed. The full specific-code detail follows.
