What Birkman Tells Us About the Men of ATO

June 2026  ·  Birkman Method  ·  ATO Staff
Membership Insight
What fifteen years of Birkman data shows about the men coming into ATO, who steps up to lead, and how to work with them

The Birkman Method reads a man three ways. How he comes across to the people around him. What he needs from those people to do his best work. And how he behaves under stress when that need goes unmet. For most of our history those readings lined up, and you could trust the surface to tell you what a man needed.

Across the last fifteen years of members, they have pulled apart. This brief lays out three things: what is changing in the men, who among them steps up to lead, and what both mean for how we coach chapters and work with officers. It is built from the Birkman profiles of roughly 6,400 members grouped by year of birth, with a separate comparison of officers and non-officers. Figures appear where they show the size of a difference.

A change over time
1How the men are changing
Comparing members by year of birth across the last fifteen years, two things have clearly moved. What a young man needs from people has pulled away from how he comes across, and what energizes him has shifted toward the practical.
The gap between how they seem and what they need has widened

Younger members present as more straightforward, more team-first, and more easygoing than members did a decade ago. Underneath that surface, their need has moved the other way. They need more genuine affirmation, more individual recognition, and clearer structure and authority. They also need more room to think before deciding and more outlet for what they feel.

The widest of these gaps is the need for respect and affirmation, which rose more than any other across the cohorts. The practical effect is simple. A young man's surface increasingly misleads you about what he needs, and the instinct to respond to what you see points you the wrong way.

What energizes them has shifted toward the practical

Interests are the Birkman measure of what motivates a man, and they moved more than anything else in the data. The pull is toward the concrete and the hands-on, and away from the intellectual, the artistic, and the service-oriented.

They present a more managed image

Image management, the degree to which a man answers in a way that puts his best face forward, rose about nine points across the cohorts. Younger members are more conscious of how they come across and more careful about it. Together with the changes above, this means a man's outward style is a weaker guide to what he needs than it was for earlier cohorts.

The service gap to watch

Interest in serving and helping others is declining while the need to receive support is rising. These men need support more than they are drawn to provide it. Building a service and brotherhood culture now requires more deliberate effort, because interest in serving others is weaker than in earlier cohorts.

A difference in structure
2Who steps up, and who fits which seat
A different question, with no time axis. Among the roughly 5,800 men past their undergraduate years who have a Birkman profile, who became an officer, and does the role fit the man.
How officers differ from non-officers

Officers differ from non-officers by degree rather than kind. On average they are more outgoing, more active, quicker to decide, more organized, and more conscious of how they come across. The differences are small and statistically consistent, not large enough to identify a future officer from a Birkman profile. Read them as group tendencies, not as a screening tool.

One result stands out. Officers score highest on the hidden affirmation need. They present as capable and self-assured while needing more respect and validation than non-officers. Because they present as able to take blunt feedback, they are the men most likely to receive it, and also the men who most need affirmation alongside it.

Men sort into the role that fits them

Split the officers by role and a clear pattern appears. Men sort into the role whose work matches their interests. The sharpest signal in the analysis is the Treasurer, whose interest in numbers and detail stands well above the other officers. The roles differ in the profile that fits them. The pills mark the Birkman interests each role pulls toward, in their Map-quadrant colors. The President carries none, because that role is distinguished by presence and decisiveness rather than a single interest.

The application
3What this means for coaching
The findings above share one implication for coaching. A man's needs often differ from how he presents, so coaching is more effective when it targets the need.
The one thing to carry into every chapter

Most stress behavior in a chapter reflects an unmet need rather than a character problem. An officer who goes evasive, refuses feedback, or digs in is usually responding to a need that went unread, because his surface style pointed elsewhere. Diagnose the need before responding to the behavior.

Three shifts in how to coach
Shift One Self-Consciousness
The straightforward man needs more affirmation, not less
What you seeDirect, matter-of-fact, clear answers, seems at ease and able to take it straight.
What he needsGenuine respect, encouragement, criticism balanced with real acknowledgment, words chosen with care.

The typical young member presents as direct and unbothered, so the instinct is to be blunt back, skip the encouragement, and move straight to the correction. That instinct is increasingly wrong. When he gets blunt correction without affirmation, he does not push back. He goes quiet, gets evasive, and pulls away, and coaches read that as disengagement. It is an unmet need for respect. Pair every correction with genuine acknowledgment, and be explicit that you respect his role and his work.

By the numbersAcross the cohorts, the need for affirmation rose about 14 points, the largest move of any need in the data, while the straightforward surface barely shifted, so the difference between presented style and underlying need roughly doubled.
Shift Two Incentives
Recognize the man by name, not only the chapter
What you seeTeam-first language, talks about the brotherhood and the group, plays down his own part.
What he needsTo be recognized as an individual for what he personally did, by name.

Younger members talk in terms of the chapter and the group, so the instinct is to recognize the chapter as a unit. Group praise alone underfeeds them. The man who carried the recruitment push needs to hear his own name, not only that the chapter did well. Build individual acknowledgment into how you coach officers and how chapters hand out recognition.

By the numbersThe need for individual recognition rose about 6 points across the cohorts, while the team-first surface held steady. Their presented style understates how much individual recognition they need.
Shift Three Assertiveness
Give clear authority to the man who looks easygoing
What you seeAgreeable, low-key, less forceful than officers used to be. Looks flexible and easy to work with.
What he needsClear lines of authority, defined expectations, firm direction, room to debate issues openly.

Younger officers come across as more agreeable and less forceful, so the instinct is to read that as low ownership, or to hand them loose, open-ended direction because they seem flexible. Both misread the man. Vague direction and conflict-avoidant advising frustrate him, and when that need goes unmet, the easygoing officer becomes argumentative or dug-in under stress. Give younger officers concrete expectations, clear authority, and a setting where issues get debated in the open.

By the numbersYounger men present about 6 points less forceful than a decade ago, while their need for clear, firm authority rose about 4 points. In earlier cohorts the two were close; in recent cohorts the need exceeds the presented style.
What this changes in the room

On a chapter visit or a one-on-one with an officer, these are the habits to bring, and the habits to coach into advisors and older members, who interact with these men daily and whose instincts need recalibrating most.

  • Lead with genuine respect and acknowledgment before correction, even with men who look like they can take it straight.
  • Name individuals when you recognize work. Tie the praise to the specific thing the person did, not only to the chapter.
  • Give officers defined authority and concrete expectations rather than open-ended encouragement.
  • When an officer goes quiet, evasive, or suddenly argumentative, treat it as a signal of an unmet need. Ask what he needs before pushing harder.
  • Place men in roles that match their interests, for example the numbers-oriented man at Treasurer and the persuader at Recruitment.
  • Do not rely on a quiet, social, or easygoing surface to judge what a man needs, since surface and need are further apart in recent cohorts.

How to read this

Part One compares the Birkman profiles of roughly 6,400 members grouped by year of birth, from 1992 through 2006. Part Two compares roughly 5,800 men past their undergraduate years, split by whether they ever held a chapter office.

The differences are statistically reliable and modest in size. They describe average differences between groups and do not predict individual behavior. No single profile predicts whether a man will lead or which role he will hold.

Year of birth is correlated with when a man took Birkman and his age at the time, so part of the change over time could reflect life stage rather than a generational shift. The coaching implications are the same in either case.
