Elevating the Alumni Volunteer Experience

FEA Annual Meeting 2026  ·  Session Summary

This summary captures key frameworks, data, and implications from the FEA 2026 keynote on alumni volunteerism, presented by Tobi Johnson of Tobi Johnson and Associates LLC. The session drew on research from NCVO, the American Society of Association Executives, McKinley Advisors, and VolunteerPro to make the case that volunteer engagement is a designed experience - not a default outcome of organizational loyalty. The frameworks below are directly applicable to how ATO structures, supports, and sustains its alumni volunteer relationships.

Leadership in a Changing World: Adapting Today's Strategy to Volunteerism — Tobi Johnson, Tobi Johnson and Associates LLC
The Macro Environment

Johnson opened by framing the current moment as a "trust recession" and "relationship recession." Post-pandemic isolation, declining institutional confidence, and rising demands on personal time have converged into a structural pressure on volunteer-involving organizations. Alumni are not less generous than prior generations - they are more deliberate about where they invest their time. Organizations that treat volunteer engagement as a loyalty transaction rather than a value exchange will continue to see declining participation regardless of recruitment effort.

The McKinley Advisors 2022 Association Viewpoint found that only 28 percent of association leaders were very confident their current business model would support their mission over the next three to five years. The primary barrier to innovation cited was not funding or strategy - it was resistance to change from staff and volunteer leaders. For fraternal organizations with 160-year institutional histories, this finding is pointed.

The broader sector data confirm this is not an ATO-specific challenge. In the VolunteerPro 2024 Volunteer Management Progress Report, 43 percent of volunteer managers in open-ended responses named either attracting or keeping volunteers as their single biggest challenge - and retention returned to the top five challenges for the first time in two years, driven by waning commitment, no-shows, and failed transitions from training to active service. The pressure is structural, not cyclical.

The Real Reasons Volunteers Decline

The most commonly stated objection - "I don't have time" - is rarely the actual reason for non-participation. It is a socially acceptable exit that protects the relationship and avoids conflict. Because organizations hear "no time," they optimize for scheduling flexibility and recruitment volume rather than addressing the underlying drivers.

What They Say What They Actually Mean
"I don't have time" Any of the following six things - but rarely the time itself.
Reason 1
I don't see enough value to prioritize this over other demands on my time.
Reason 2
The role is not interesting to me. It feels like a task list, not an opportunity.
Reason 3
I don't understand what's expected of me or what success looks like in the role.
Reason 4
I'm not confident I could do the job well and don't want to fail publicly.
Reason 5
I don't believe I'd be welcomed or that I belong in this community anymore.
Reason 6
I have a complicated history with this organization or its leadership.

Because dissatisfied volunteers rarely state their real reasons for leaving, the organization loses the feedback signal and never addresses the actual problem. Negative word of mouth follows - and in a fraternal alumni network with decades of relationships, that signal travels fast and wide.

Moving a volunteer from dissatisfied to satisfied is a 3x improvement in retention. The gap between satisfied and very satisfied is smaller but still meaningful. The intervention point is experience design - not recruitment volume.

The Flywheel Model

Johnson contrasted the traditional volunteer funnel - recruit, use, repeat - with a flywheel model in which engagement produces delight, and delight produces attraction through word of mouth. The flywheel is self-reinforcing when the experience is strong, and self-defeating when it is not. In an alumni network the size of ATO's, the word-of-mouth surface area is enormous in both directions.

Source: Tobi Johnson and Associates LLC – FEA Annual Meeting 2026

Word of mouth is the turbocharge for the flywheel - and it only runs in the right direction when current volunteers are telling others the role is worth their time. Organizations that design for this explicitly - through ambassador programs, journey design, and peer recruitment - outperform those that rely on institutional loyalty as a substitute.

2024 VolunteerPro Data

Despite persistent challenges, sector sentiment is improving. The 2024 Volunteer Management Progress Report found that 51.6 percent of volunteer managers expressed happy or extremely happy sentiments - up from 46.2 percent the prior year. Organizations that invest in the volunteer experience are beginning to feel traction. Those that do not are falling further behind the organizations that have.

Three Core Human Needs

Drawing on behavioral research, Johnson organized the volunteer experience around three core human needs that must all be present for a role to produce retention and advocacy. Satisfying two of three is not sufficient.

Safety
Avoiding Harm
Emotional safety, physical safety, and psychological safety. Volunteers need to know they can ask questions, admit uncertainty, and surface problems without consequence.
Satisfaction
Approaching Rewards
Sense of purpose, visible progress, and meaningful feedback. Volunteers who cannot see whether their work is making a difference lose the progress signal quickly.
Connection
Belonging
Attaching to others, friendship, and a genuine sense of being welcomed. Fraternal organizations have a natural advantage here - but only if the organizational relationship is healthy enough for alumni to claim it.

Psychological safety sits underneath all three but is most fragile in organizational contexts where alumni have experienced dismissal, overruling, or institutional tension. If that history is present in any form, it suppresses candor across the entire volunteer network - not only among the individuals who had direct negative experiences.

Volunteer UX: Designing the Experience

Johnson introduced the concept of "Volunteer UX" - the idea that every touchpoint in the volunteer relationship is a designed experience, not an accident of institutional habit. Applying the Nielsen Norman Group definition of user experience: does every interaction make the role easier, clearer, and more meaningful? Or does it add friction, confusion, and burden?

The primary design principle Johnson emphasized: how can we make volunteering easier, not harder? Most organizations do the opposite by default - adding compliance requirements, training mandates, and reporting obligations without asking whether each addition is worth the volunteer's time and attention.

Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Rewards

Research consistently shows volunteers are more motivated by intrinsic rewards than extrinsic ones. Plaques and pins do not drive retention. The following design elements produce intrinsic satisfaction:

  • Communicate what impact is occurring - close the feedback loop so volunteers can see their effect
  • Offer flexibility in how the role is fulfilled to accommodate individual circumstances
  • Cut unnecessary bureaucracy - every administrative friction point erodes willingness
  • Build in social time and connection across the volunteer community
  • Ensure person-task fit - the right person in the right role
  • Do not coerce volunteers beyond their stated limitations
  • Focus on inclusivity and belonging at every touchpoint
The Warm Welcome

Johnson identified a warm welcome as one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost interventions available to volunteer-involving organizations. The welcome is not simply a greeting - it must simultaneously establish clarity (what is this role, what is expected of me) and belonging (I am welcome here, this community is mine). Most onboarding achieves one but not both. When clarity is present without belonging, volunteers become compliant but disengaged. When belonging is present without clarity, volunteers become enthusiastic but ineffective.

The source of the welcome matters as much as whether it happens. A form email from a staff coordinator carries different weight than a personal call from a peer volunteer or a chapter alumnus. The warmth must be genuine and the messenger must carry relational credibility.

Four Pillars of Integrated Onboarding

Johnson drew on ASAE research to present a four-pillar onboarding framework that moves beyond compliance and training toward a relational model. The business case: reduce replacement costs, reduce loss of institutional knowledge, accelerate time to competency, and raise the average productivity level across the volunteer cohort.

Interpersonal Connections

  • Mentor relationship with an experienced peer
  • Connections among volunteer cohort members
  • Consistent, named staff relationship

Organizational Culture

  • Mission, vision, and values - not assumed, stated
  • Role clarity before the volunteer begins
  • Talking points that connect the role to organizational goals

Early Training and Support

  • Begins on day one, not after the first problem
  • Personal development framing, not just compliance
  • Bite-size content sequenced over time

Exposure to Strategy

  • Share program goals with volunteers, not just staff
  • Disclose in layers over time as the relationship develops
  • Gather insights from volunteers - they are end-users of the system

The "disclose in layers" principle under Exposure to Strategy is a sequencing argument: the relationship earns depth. Front-loading everything at orientation creates overwhelm and compliance rather than genuine understanding. Strategy should flow into the volunteer relationship over months, not minutes.

2024 VolunteerPro Data

Over half of volunteer managers across the sector - 52 percent - operate without clear strategic direction from their organization, either because volunteer engagement is absent from the strategic plan or because managers are unaware of what the plan contains. The "Exposure to Strategy" pillar addresses a gap that is nearly universal in volunteer-involving organizations.

Email Nurture for Volunteer Onboarding

Johnson presented an automated email sequence as a practical vehicle for the integrated onboarding framework. The sequence operationalizes "disclose in layers" by spreading information, relationship-building, and impact communication over 90 days rather than front-loading at orientation.

  • Day 1: Warm welcome
  • Day 2: Organizational story and history
  • Day 4: Mission in action - a concrete example of impact
  • Day 7: Volunteer path highlight - what success looks like in this role
  • Day 10: Upcoming events and calendar
  • Day 14: Check-in from staff
  • Day 18: "Did you know" - organizational context or history
  • Day 22: Success story from a peer volunteer or chapter
  • Day 26: Future opportunities and development paths
  • Day 45: Next steps check-in
  • Day 60: Mission survey - gather volunteer feedback
  • Day 90: Testimonial request - ask the volunteer to share their experience

The sequence is triggered by role assignment in the organization's CRM and requires no manual action from staff once built. It maintains the organizational relationship during the periods when direct staff contact is absent - which is where most new volunteers lose confidence or interest.

A Structural Differentiator: The BOT Model

Most volunteer-involving organizations have not solved the volunteer leadership problem. The 2024 VolunteerPro report found that one-third of organizations engage zero volunteers in leadership roles supervising or guiding other volunteers, and another 39.5 percent engage only 1-5 percent of their volunteer workforce in any leadership capacity. The median engagement in volunteer leadership is effectively near zero across the sector.

ATO's Board of Trustees model places alumni directly in coaching and oversight roles at the chapter level. That is not a common program design - it is a structural commitment to volunteer leadership that most organizations have never made. The question is not whether the model is worth having. It is whether the infrastructure around the model matches the ambition of it. The onboarding, support, strategy exposure, and feedback mechanisms that Johnson's frameworks describe are exactly what determines whether volunteer leaders produce impact or quietly disengage.

2024 VolunteerPro Data

The 2024 report found a correlation between the percentage of time volunteer managers devoted to engagement and the proportion of volunteers holding leadership roles. When staff have dedicated time to nurture volunteer talent, volunteer leaders are more likely to emerge and remain. The inverse is also true: when volunteer management is a fractional duty, leadership pipelines do not develop.

Questions for Jacob to Answer

The frameworks from this session point to a set of concrete questions about how the BOT program currently operates. The answers will determine where the highest-leverage improvements are. These are not rhetorical - each one has a specific operational implication depending on the answer.

Warm Welcome

When a new BOT member is confirmed, what happens in the first 48 hours? Who reaches out, what do they say, and does it connect the role to brotherhood or only to tasks?

Role Clarity

Does every BOT member receive a written description of their specific role before they begin? Does it define what success looks like, not just what the responsibilities are?

Interpersonal Connections

Are BOT members connected to other BOT members outside their own chapter? Is there any facilitated peer network, cohort, or mentorship pairing in place?

Exposure to Strategy

Do BOT members know what the National Fraternity's goals are for their chapter this year? Do they see how their work connects to chapter health metrics?

Early Training and Support

Is there a structured experience in the first 90 days, or does support happen as-needed when a BOT member reaches out? Who bears the burden of identifying gaps?

Impact Communication

How does a BOT member find out whether their chapter improved? Do they receive any feedback on whether their coaching made a difference, and from whom?

Psychological Safety

If a BOT member's chapter is struggling, do they feel comfortable surfacing that with staff? Or does the reporting structure make candor feel risky?

The 2024 VolunteerPro report found that leadership support rating was the strongest single predictor of volunteer manager satisfaction and intent to stay: 84.9 percent of those who rated leadership support at five stars were satisfied or very satisfied, versus 17.2 percent of those who rated it at one star. The same pattern held for retention. The quality of the National Fraternity's relationship with BOT members is not peripheral to the program - it is the program.

Person-Task Fit

Beyond the interview, what informs BOT placement decisions? Is there any structured assessment of whether a candidate's strengths match the specific demands of the role?

Asking the Core Question

Has ATO ever directly asked BOT members whether serving was worth their time - and if not, what has stopped us from asking?
BOT Survey Deployment Roadmap

The BOT coaching reports - built from survey data and benchmarked against True Merit chapter performance - are the most immediately deployable tool for the Exposure to Strategy pillar. The reports exist. The deployment model is the open question. Below is a starting framework for fall rollout.

Phase 1  ·  Pre-Deployment

Prepare the Reports and the Conversation

  • Confirm True Merit benchmark data is current and the report format is finalized
  • Identify which chapters have BOT chairman survey data vs. proxy data from the chapter president - non-completion is itself a signal and warrants higher-priority outreach
  • Develop a coaching conversation framework for the Director of Alumni Services: what questions to ask, how to surface gaps without creating defensiveness, how to end with one agreed-upon focus area
  • Draft talking points connecting report findings to chapter health outcomes that BOT members care about
Phase 2  ·  Initial Delivery

Conversations, Not Document Drops

  • Schedule a 30-minute call with each BOT chairman before sending the report - the conversation provides context for the data
  • Frame the report as a coaching tool, not an evaluation: the goal is identifying one gap to close this semester, not grading performance
  • Walk through the True Merit benchmark comparison together on the call
  • Send the report after the call, with a follow-up note connecting the data to what was discussed
Phase 3  ·  Onboarding Sequence

Build the 90-Day Welcome Campaign

  • Design a 12-touch email sequence triggered by BOT assignment in Salesforce
  • Draw content from existing materials: the organizational story, coaching report insights, True Merit chapter success narratives, and mission-connection language
  • The BOT coaching reports serve as the Exposure to Strategy content layer within the sequence
  • Monitor open rates and engagement quarterly - adjust based on what is and is not landing
Phase 4  ·  Measure and Iterate

Close the Loop

  • Track BOT chairman retention year-over-year as the primary outcome metric
  • Survey BOT members at end of academic year: was this worth your time? What would have made it better?
  • Connect BOT engagement data to chapter Five Vitals performance - test whether more engaged BOTs produce stronger chapters
  • Use findings to refine the report, the conversation framework, and the email sequence annually

Questions for ATO to Answer

1
Are we asking whether the experience is worth their time? ATO does not currently collect systematic feedback from BOT members on their experience quality. Building that feedback mechanism is the prerequisite for every other improvement.
2
Are we designing for psychological safety? An advisor who fears asking for help or admitting a chapter is struggling will not be effective. The 2024 VolunteerPro data establish that the quality of organizational support is the strongest predictor of volunteer satisfaction and retention. Psychological safety is not assumed by shared identity - it must be explicitly modeled in every staff interaction.
3
Is the BOT role designed or inherited? Most role expectations have accreted over time rather than being intentionally designed. A structured role clarity exercise - what does this role require, what does success look like, what is out of scope - would produce immediate gains in both satisfaction and retention.
4
Are BOT members connected to each other? Peer networks among advisors do not form automatically. Intentional facilitation - regional cohorts, cross-chapter conversations, experienced mentors for new advisors - is required to produce the connection need the research identifies as essential.
5
Are we deploying BOT coaching reports as conversations or as documents? The reports exist. The deployment model will determine whether they produce insight and coaching relationships - or whether they land in an inbox and go unread.
